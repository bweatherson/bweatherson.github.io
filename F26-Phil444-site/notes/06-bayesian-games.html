6  Bayesian Games

6.1 Two Kinds of Ignorance

In the games of Chapter 5, players were sometimes ignorant about what had already happened. When a player has to move without knowing which node of the tree they are at, we represent that with an information set, and we say the game is one of imperfect information. Prisoners’ Dilemma in extensive form is like this. The second player moves knowing the rules, knowing the payoffs, knowing everything about the game, and not knowing only what the first player just did.

There is a different thing a player might not know. They might not know what the game is. They might not know what the other player’s payoffs are, and so not know what the other player is trying to achieve. Games like this are games of incomplete information.

The distinction matters and it is easy to lose, so it is worth putting the two side by side. In a game of imperfect information you know the whole payoff table and you don’t know where you are in the tree. In a game of incomplete information you don’t know the payoff table.

Almost every interesting application of game theory outside the classroom involves incomplete information. When you bargain over a used car you do not know how much the seller values it. When a firm decides whether to enter a market it does not know the incumbent’s costs. When two states approach a crisis, neither knows how much the other is willing to lose. Writing down a payoff table and assuming both players can see it is a serious idealisation.

But we don’t obviously have a theory for these cases. Our solution concepts all take a game as input, and a game is a payoff table. If the players don’t know the payoff table, we cannot even say what game they are playing, and if we cannot say that, we cannot say what an equilibrium of it would be. Worse, there is a regress waiting. If Row doesn’t know Col’s payoffs, Row has beliefs about them. Col then has beliefs about Row’s beliefs about Col’s payoffs. And Row has beliefs about those. It looks like modelling ignorance about payoffs requires an infinite hierarchy of beliefs about beliefs, which is not something we can write on a page.

6.2 Types

John Harsanyi (1967) found a clever way through this. Games of incomplete information can be turned into games of imperfect information, which we already know how to handle.

The trick is to let the ignorance be about a move rather than about the game. We add a new player, Nature, who moves first, and whose move settles what the payoffs are. Nature’s move is not a choice; it is a draw from a probability distribution that is common knowledge. The players then observe part of what Nature did, and not all of it.

What each player observes about Nature’s move is called their type. A type settles everything about a player that the other players might be unsure of. In most of our examples the type just is the player’s payoff function, but nothing turns on that. A type could encode how patient a player is, or how good they are at chess, or what they believe about the other player. It could encode something completely distinct; in a scenario where one player can observe whether it is raining and the other can’t, it’s sometimes useful to classify the first player’s type as rain or sun. (If you insist, you could call their type sees rain or sees sun, but we often use the externally individuated types.)

So a Bayesian game has:

  • a set of players;
  • for each player, a set of actions;
  • for each player, a set of possible types;
  • a probability distribution over the profiles of types, called the prior;
  • for each player, a payoff function that depends on the actions of everyone and on their own type.

We assume the prior is common knowledge. This is the common prior assumption, and without it we need a more complicated theory. One consequence of it is that the players start out agreeing about how likely each type profile is, and disagree only because they have seen different draws. That assumption is not always plausible in the cases the these tools are applied to, and it’s often worth questioning on an application. For now, notice one useful theoretical simplification that this assumption gets us. Instead of thinking about a potentially infinite hierarchy of beleifs about beliefs, we can just use the conditional probabilities a player gets from the prior, given their own type, and everyone can compute those.

6.3 Bayesian Nash Equilibrium

Once the game has been transformed this way, we can reuse Nash equilibrium almost unchanged. There is one adjustment to make first.

In a Bayesian game, a player knows their own type before acting. So a strategy cannot just be an action; it has to say what the player does for each type they might turn out to have. A strategy in a Bayesian game is a function from that player’s types to that player’s actions.

This is a common mistake, so it’s worth belaboring the point. If Col has two types, and Col has two moves regardless of their type, then Col has four strategies, not two: do X whatever happens, do Y whatever happens, do X if the first type and Y if the second, or do Y if the first and X if the second. Col will only ever perform one action in any play of the game. But the strategy has to be defined for the type Col didn’t turn out to have, because Row’s reasoning depends on what Col would have done in that case.

A Bayesian Nash equilibrium is then a profile of strategies, one for each player, such that each type of each player is choosing an action that maximises their expected payoff, given the strategies of the others and given the beliefs about the others’ types that they get by conditioning the prior on their own type.

Equivalently, and this is the cleaner way to say it: a Bayesian Nash equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium of the imperfect information game that Harsanyi’s transformation produces. In a sense, this isn’t a new solution concept; it’s a new term for an old concept.

6.4 An Example

Take Battle of the Sexes from Section 1.6, and make one change. Row still wants to meet Col. But Row is no longer sure that Col wants to meet Row.

Col has two types. With probability \(\nicefrac{1}{2}\) Col is sociable, and wants to meet, with the payoffs we saw before. With probability \(\nicefrac{1}{2}\) Col is elusive, and would rather be wherever Row is not. Col knows which type Col is. Row does not.

If Col is sociable, the game (shown in Table 6.1) is the one we already know.

If Col is elusive, Row’s payoffs are unchanged, but Col’s change.

In Table 6.2, look first at Col, who has it easy, because Col knows which table is ‘real’.

Sociable Col wants to match Row. If Row plays X, sociable Col gets 1 from X and 0 from Y, so plays X. If Row plays Y, sociable Col gets 0 from X and 2 from Y, so plays Y.

Elusive Col wants to mismatch. If Row plays X, elusive Col gets 0 from X and 2 from Y, so plays Y. If Row plays Y, elusive Col gets 1 from X and 0 from Y, so plays X.

Now Row, who has to average over the two. Suppose Col’s strategy is play X if sociable, Y if elusive. Then Col plays X half the time and Y half the time, from Row’s point of view, and Row’s expected payoffs are

\[ E(X) = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 0 = 1 \]

\[ E(Y) = \frac{1}{2} \times 0 + \frac{1}{2} \times 1 = \frac{1}{2} \]

So Row plays X. And we should check this is consistent: given that Row plays X, sociable Col does want to play X, and elusive Col does want to play Y. Nothing unravels, so we have a Bayesian Nash equilibrium.

Two things about this are worth spelling out in more detail.

The first is that Col’s two types do different things. The possibility of this happening is why the type system is interesting. Externally, Row is facing one person. But from the inside, from Row’s perspective, they are facing a distribution over opponents, and has to pick an action that is good on average against it.

The second is that Row’s behaviour looks, from the outside, exactly like behaviour in an ordinary game. Row picks X and sticks with it. The incomplete information is not visible in what Row does. But it is visible in why Row does it, and in what Row would have done had the probabilities been different. Suppose Col were sociable with probability \(p\) rather than \(\nicefrac{1}{2}\). Then Row’s expected payoff from X is \(2p\) and from Y is \(1-p\), so the equilibrium we just found survives exactly when \(p \geqslant \frac{1}{3}\). Below that it falls apart, though something strange starts to happen. There is no pure strategy equilibrium when \(p\) is below \(frac{1}{3}\). Whatever Row does, if Col knows it, they will avoid Row, which Row doesn’t want. So Row has to mix, playing X a third of the time, and Row and Col still find each other sometimes, by luck rather than design.

6.5 Purification

We can now settle a debt from Section 3.8.

The problem there was that mixed equilibria seem to require players to randomise, and it is not clear why anyone would. At a mixed equilibrium every pure strategy in the support does exactly as well as the mixture, so nothing recommends the mixture over any of them. The only thing a player’s mixture accomplishes is keeping the other player indifferent, which is a strange thing for a self-interested agent to be aiming at.

Harsanyi’s response is that the randomness is really in ignorance of the payoffs, not in what the other player does (Harsanyi 1973). Here’s the idea. Imagine that we’re in a game with a mixed strategy equilibrium and no pure strategy equilibrium. And each player has a small amount of private information. They know their own payoffs. For instance, in one outcome they might know that their payoff is 2, while the other player only knows that it is \(2 + \delta\), where \(\delta\) is randomly drawn from \([-\epsilon, \epsilon]\), for arbitrarily small \(\epsilon\). Now we’re playing a Bayesian game; the player’s type is the value of \(\delta\).

In the Bayesian equilibrium of the perturbed game, almost every type has a strict best reply. A player whose private draw tips them slightly towards one action simply takes it. Almost no one randomises; almost every type plays a pure strategy. But an opponent who cannot see the draw cannot predict the action, and from the opponent’s point of view the player’s behaviour is a probability distribution over actions.

Harsanyi’s theorem (1973) is that as \(\epsilon\) shrinks to zero, that of pure strategies converges to the mixed equilibrium of the original game. The mixed equilibrium is the limit of pure strategy Bayesian Nash equilibria of nearby games of incomplete information.

If that is right, the interpretive problem dissolves. Nobody was ever randomising. Each player was doing the strictly best thing their own private situation called for, and the mixture was never a description of anyone’s decision. It was a description of what the other player could work out.

Whether it is right is a further question, and there are two obvious worries. The first is that this all rests on reasoning from a sequence to what happens in the limit. You might worry that this is illegitimate. Relatedly, you might worry that we can’t tell much about a game by looking at different games, even if they are neighbors. For what it’s worth, the idea that we should look at nearby games has been really influential. We’re not going to focus on it, but the formal statements of many of the concepts we’ll somewhat informally introduce in the rest of this chapter uses the same idea.

6.6 Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium

The core idea behind subgame perfect equilibrium was that we wanted to eliminate equilibria that relied on ‘incredible threats’. That is, there are some equilibria such that the first player makes a certain move only because if they make a different move, the second player to move would, on their current strategy, do something that makes things worse for both players. But there’s no reason to think that the second player would actually do that.

The same kind of consideration can arise in games where there aren’t any subgames. For instance, consider the following game, which we’ll call Perfect.

The game here is a little different to one we’ve seen so far. First Player 1 makes a move, either \(L\), \(M\) or \(R\). If her move is \(R\), the game ends, with the (2, 2) payout. If she moves \(L\) or \(M\), the game continues with a move by Player 2. But crucially, Player 2 does not know what move Player 1 made, so she does not know which node she is at. So there isn’t a subgame here to start. Player 2 chooses \(l\) or \(r\), and then the game ends.

There are two Nash equilibria to Perfect. The obvious equilibrium is \(Ll\). In that equilibrium  Player 1 gets her maximal payout, and Player 2 gets as much as she can get given that the rightmost node is unobtainable. But there is another Nash equilibrium available, namely \(Rr\). In that equilibrium, Player 2 gets her maximal payout, and Player 1 gets the most she can get given that Player 2 is playing \(r\). But note what a strange equilibrium it is. It relies on the idea that Player 2 would play \(r\) were she to be given a move. But that is absurd. Once she gets a move, she has a straight choice between 1, if she plays \(l\), and 0, if she plays \(r\). So obviously she’ll play \(l\).

This is just like the examples that we used to motivate subgame perfect equilibrium, but that concept doesn’t help us here. So we need a new concept. The core idea is that each player should be modelled as a Bayesian expected utility maximiser. More formally, the following constraints are put on players.

  1. At each point in the game, each player has a probability distribution over where they are in the game. These probability distributions are correct about the other players’ actions. That is, if a player is playing a strategy \(S\), everyone has probability 1 that they are playing \(S\). If \(S\) is a mixed strategy, this might involve having probabilities between 0 and 1 in propositions about which move the other player will make, but players have correct beliefs about other players’ strategies.
  2. No matter which node is reach, each player is disposed to maximise expected utility on arrival at that node.
  3. When a player had a positive probability of arriving at a node, on reaching that node they update by conditionalisation.
  4. When a player gave 0 probability to reaching a node (e.g., because the equilibrium being played did not include that node), they have some disposition or other to form a set of consistent beliefs at that node.

The last constraint is very weak, but it does enough to eliminate the equilibrium \(Rr\). The constraint implies that when Player 2 moves, she must have some probability distribution \(\Pr\) such that there’s an \(x\) such that \(\Pr(L) = x\) and \(\Pr(M) = 1-x\). Whatever value \(x\) takes, the expected utility of \(l\) given \(\Pr\) is 1, and the expected utility of \(r\) is 0. So being disposed to play \(r\) violates the second condition. So \(Rr\) is not a Perfect Bayesian equilibrium.

It’s true, but I’m not going to prove it, that all Perfect Bayesian equilibria are Nash equilibria. It’s also true that the converse does not hold, and this we have proved; Perfect is an example.

6.7 Signaling Games

Concepts like Perfect Bayesian equilibrium are useful for the broad class of games known as signaling games. In a signaling game, Player 1 gets some information that is hidden from player 2. Many applications of these games involve the information being de se information, so the information that Player 1 gets is referred to as her type. But that’s inessential; what is essential is that only one player gets this information. Player 1 then has a choice of move to make. There is a small loss of generality here, but we’ll restrict our attention to games where Player 1’s choices are independent of her type, i.e., of the information she receives. Player 2 sees Player 1’s move (but not, remember, her type) and then has a choice of her own to make. Again with a small loss of generality, we’ll restrict attention to cases where Player 2’s available moves are independent of what Player 1 does.

We’ll start, in game Simple Signal with a signaling game where the parties’ interests are perfectly aligned.

As above, we use an empty disc to signal the initial node of the game tree. In this case, it is the node in the centre of the tree. The first move is made by Nature, again denoted as \(N\). Nature assigns a type to Player 1; that is, she makes some proposition true, and reveals it to Player 1. Call that proposition \(q\). We’ll say that Nature moves left if \(q\) is true, and right otherwise. We assume the probability (in some good sense of probability) of \(q\) is \(p\), and this is known before the start of the game. After Nature moves, Player 1 has to choose \(U\)p or \(D\)own. Player 2 is shown Player 1’s choice, but not Nature’s move. That’s the effect of the horizontal dashed lines. If the game reaches one of the upper nodes, Player 2 doesn’t know which one it is, and if it reaches one of the lower nodes, again Player 2 doesn’t know which it is. Then Player 2 has a make a choice, here simply denoted as \(l\)eft or \(r\)ight.

In any game of this form, each player has a choice of four strategies. Player 1 has to choose what to do if \(q\) is true, and what to do if \(q\) is false. We’ll write \(\alpha \beta\) for the strategy of doing \(\alpha\) if \(q\), and \(\beta\) if \(\neg q\). Since \(\alpha\) could be \(U\) or \(D\), and \(\beta\) could be \(U\) or \(D\), there are four possible strategies. Player 2 has to choose what to do if \(U\) is played, and what to do if \(D\) is played. We’ll write \(\gamma \delta\) for the strategy of doing \(\gamma\) if \(U\) is played, and \(\delta\) if \(D\) is played. Again, there are four possible choices. (If Player 2 knew what move Nature had made, there would be four degrees of freedom in her strategy choice, so she’d have 16 possible strategies. Fortunately, she doesn’t have that much flexibility!)

Any game of this broad form is a signaling game. signaling games differ in (a) the interpretation of the moves, and (b) the payoffs. Simple Signal has a very simple payoff structure. Both players get 1 if Player 2 moves \(l\) iff \(q\), and 0 otherwise. If we think of \(l\) as the formation of a belief that \(q\), and \(r\) as the formation of the opposite belief, this becomes a simple communication game. The players get a payoff iff Player 2 ends up with a true belief about \(q\), so Player 1 is trying to communicate to Player 2 whether \(q\) is true. This kind of simple communication game was used by David Lewis (1969) to show that game theoretic approaches could be fruitful in the study of meaning. The game is perfectly symmetric if \(p = \nicefrac{1}{2}\); so as to introduce some asymmetries, I’ll work through the case where \(p = \nicefrac{3}{5}\).

Simple Signal has a dizzying array of Nash equilibria, even given this asymmetry introducing assumption. They include the following:

  • There are two separating equilibria, where what Player 1 does depends on what Nature does. These are \(\langle UD, lr \rangle\) and \(\langle DU, rl \rangle\). These are rather nice equilibria; both players are guaranteed to get their maximal payout.
  • There are two pooling equilibria, where what Player 1 does is independent of what Nature does. These are \(\langle UU, ll \rangle\) and \(\langle DD, ll \rangle\). Given that she gets no information from Player 1, Player 2 may as well guess. And since \(\Pr(q) > \nicefrac{1}{2}\), she should guess that \(q\) is true; i.e., she should play \(l\). And given that Player 2 is going to guess, Player 1 may as well play anything. So these are also equilibria.
  • And there are some babbling equilibria. For instance, there is the equilibrium where Player 1 plays either \(UU\) or \(DD\) with some probability \(r\), and Player 2 plays \(ll\).

Unfortunately, these are all Perfect Bayesian equilibria too. For the separating and babbling equilibria, it is easy to see that conditionalising on what Player 1 plays leads to Player 2 maximising expected utility by playing her part of the equilibrium. And for the pooling equilibria, as long as the probability of \(q\) stays above \(\nicefrac{1}{2}\) in any ‘off-the-equilibrium-path’ play (e.g., conditional on \(D\) in the \(\langle UU, ll \rangle\) equilibrium), Player 2 maximises expected utility at every node.

That doesn’t mean we have nothing to say. For one thing, the separating equilibria are Pareto-dominant in the sense that both players do better on those equilibria than they do on any other. So that’s a non-coincidental reason to think that they will be the equilibria that arise. There are other refinements on Perfect Bayesian equilibria that are more narrowly tailored to signaling games. We’ll introduce them by looking at a couple of famous signaling games.

6.8 Signaling without Cooperation

Economists have been interested for several decades in game-theoretic representations of college that give make going to college essentially a signal in a game. For example, consider the following variant of the signaling game, College Signal. It has the following intended interpretation:

  • Player 1 is a student and potential worker, Player 2 is an employer.
  • The student is either type high or low1 with probability \(p\) of being high. Nature reveals the type to the student, but only the probability to the employer, so \(p\) is that the student is high.
  • The student has the choice of going to college (\(U\)) or the beach (\(D\)).
  • The employer has the choice of hiring the student (\(l\)) or rejecting them (\(r\)).

In College Signal we make the following extra assumptions about the payoffs.

  • Being hired is worth 4 to the student.
  • Going to college rather than the beach costs the high student 1, and the low student 5, since college is much harder work for low types.
  • The employer gets no benefit from hiring college students as such.
  • Hiring a high student pays the employer 1, hiring a low student costs the employer 1, and rejections have no cost or benefit.

The resulting game tree is shown in Figure 6.3. The first move, by nature, sends high types to the left, and low types to the right. In this game, low students never prefer to go to college, since even the lure of a job doesn’t make up for the pain of actually having to study. So a rational strategy for Player 1 will never be of the form \(\alpha U\), since for low students, college is a dominated option, being dominated by \(\alpha D\). But whether high students should go to college is a trickier question. That is, it is trickier to say whether the right strategy for Player 1 is \(UD\) or \(DD\), which are the two strategies consistent with eliminating the strictly dominated strategies. (Remember that strictly dominated strategies cannot be part of a Nash equilibrium.)

First, consider the case where \(p < \nicefrac{1}{2}\). In that case, if Player 1 plays \(DD\), then Player 2 gets \(2p-1\) from playing either \(ll\) or \(rl\), and 0 from playing \(lr\) or \(rr\). So she should play one of the latter two options. If she plays \(rr\), then \(DD\) is a best response, since when employers aren’t going to hire, students prefer to go to the beach. So there is one pooling equilibrium, namely \(\langle DD, rr \rangle\). But what if Player 2 plays \(lr\)? Then Player 1’s best response is \(UD\), since high students prefer college conditional on it leading to a job. So there is also a separating equilibrium, namely \(\langle UD, lr \rangle\). The employer prefers that equilibrium, since her payoff is now \(p\) rather than 0. And students prefer it, since their payoff is \(3p\) rather than 0. So if we assume that people will end up at Pareto-dominant outcomes, we have reason to think that high students, and only high students, will go to college, and employers will hire college students, and only college students. And all this is true despite there being no advantage whatsoever to going to college in terms of how good an employee one will be.

Especially in popular treatments of the case, the existence of this kind of model can be used to motivate the idea that college only plays a signaling role. That is, some people argue that in the real world college does not make students more economically valuable, and the fact that college graduates have better employment rates, and better pay, can be explained by the signaling function of college. We’ll look in a bit at the detailed evidence for this, but first lets look at some variants of the model.

So far we assumed that \(p < \nicefrac{1}{2}\). If we drop that assumption, and assume instead that \(p > \nicefrac{1}{2}\), the case becomes more complicated. Now if Player 1 plays \(DD\), i.e., goes to the beach no matter what, Player 2’s best response is still to hire them. But note that now a very odd equilibrium becomes available. The pair \(\langle DD, rl \rangle\) is a Nash equilibrium, and, with the right assumptions, a Perfect Bayesian equilibrium. This pair says that Player 1 goes to the beach whatever her type, and Player 2 hires only beach goers.

This is a very odd strategy for Player 2 to adopt, but it is a little hard to say just why it is odd. It is clearly a Nash equilibrium. Given that Player 2 is playing \(rl\), then clearly beach-going dominates college-going. And given that Player 1 is playing \(DD\), playing \(rl\) gets as good a return as is available to Player 2, i.e., \(2p-1\). Could it also be a Perfect Bayesian equilibrium? It could, provided Player 2 has a rather odd update policy. Say that Player 2 thinks that if someone goes to college, they are low type with probability greater than \(\nicefrac{1}{2}\). That’s consistent with what we’ve said; given that Player 1 is playing \(DD\), the probability that a student goes to college is 0. So the conditional probability that a college-goer is high type is left open, and can be anything one likes in Perfect Bayesian equilibrium. So if Player 2 sets it to be, say, 0, then the rational reaction is to play \(rl\).

But now note what an odd update strategy this is for Player 2. She has to assume that if someone deviates from the \(DD\) strategy, it is someone for whom the deviation is strictly dominated. A few economists and game theorists have thought that we can put more substantive constraints on probabilities conditional on ‘off-the-equilibrium-path’ behaviour. One such constraint, is, roughly, that deviation shouldn’t lead to playing a dominated strategy. This is the “intuitive criterion” of Cho and Kreps (1987). In this game, all the criterion rules out is the odd pair \(\langle DD, rl \rangle\). It doesn’t rule out the very similiar pair \(\langle DD, ll \rangle\). But the intuitive criterion makes more substantial constraints in other games. We’ll close the discussion of the math of signaling games with such an example, and a more careful statement of the criterion.

6.9 The Intuitive Criterion

The tree in Figure 6.4 represents Canadian Signal, which is also a guessing game. The usual statement of it involves all sorts of unfortunate stereotypes about the type of men who have quiche and/or beer for breakfast, and I’m underwhelmed by it. So I’ll run with an example that relies on different stereotypes.

A North American tourist is in a bar. 60% of the North Americans who pass through that bar are from Canada, the other 40% are from the US. (This is a little implausible for most parts of the world, but maybe it is very cold climate bar. In Cho and Kreps’ version the split is 90/10 not 60/40, but all that matters is which side of 50/50 it is.) The tourist, call her Player 1, knows her nationality, although the barman doesn’t. The tourist can ask for the bar TV to be turned to hockey or to baseball, and she knows once she does that the barman will guess at her nationality. (The barman might also try to rely on her accent, but she has a fairly neutral upper-Midwest/central Canada accent.) Here are the tourist’s preferences.

  • If she is American, she has a (weak) preference for watching baseball rather than hockey.
  • If she is Canadian, she has a (weak) preference for watching hockey rather than baseball.
  • Either way, she has a strong preference for being thought of as Canadian rather than American. This preference is considerably stronger than her preference over which sport to watch.

The barman’s preferences are simpler; he prefers to make true guesses to false guesses about the tourist’s nationality. All of this is common knowledge. So the decision tree is given in Figure 6.4. In this tree, \(B\) means asking for baseball, \(H\) means asking for hockey, \(a\) means guessing the tourist is from the USA, \(c\) means guessing she is from Canada.

There are two Nash equilibria for this game. One is \(\langle HH, ca \rangle\); everyone asks for hockey, and the barman guesses Canadian if hockey, American if baseball. It isn’t too hard to check this is an equilibrium. The Canadian gets her best outcome, so it is clearly an equilibrium for her. The American gets the second-best outcome, but asking for baseball would lead to a worse outcome. And given that everyone asks for hockey, the best the barman can do is go with the prior probabilities, and that means guessing Canadian. It is easy to see how to extend this to a perfect Bayesian equilibrium; simply posit that conditional on baseball being asked for, the probability that the tourist is American is greater than \(\nicefrac{1}{2}\).

The other equilibrium is rather odder. It is \(\langle BB, ac \rangle\); everyone asks for baseball, and the barman guesses American if hockey, Canadian if baseball. Again, it isn’t too hard to see how it is Nash equilibrium. The Canadian would rather have hockey, but not at the cost of being thought American, so she has no incentive to defect. The American gets her best outcome, so she has no incentive to defect. And the barman does as well as he can given that he gets no information out of the request, since everyone requests baseball.

Surprisingly, this could also be a perfect Bayesian equilibrium. This requires that the barman maximise utility at every node. The tricky thing is to ensure he maxmises utility at the node where hockey is chosen. This can be done provided that conditional on hockey being chosen, the probability of American rises to above \(\nicefrac{1}{2}\). Well, nothing we have said rules that out, so there exists a perfect Bayesian equilibrium. But it doesn’t seem very plausible. Why would the barman adopt just this updating disposition?

There were a bunch of suggestions for formal rules to rule out intuitively crazy updating dispositions like this. I’m just going to note one of the very early attempts in this area, Cho and Kreps’ Intuitive Criterion. (Cho and Kreps 1987, 202)

Let an outcome \(o\) of the game be any combination of moves by nature and the players. Call the players’ moves collectively \(P\) and nature’s move \(N\). So an outcome is a pair \(\langle P, N \rangle\). Divide the players into three categories.

  • The happy players in \(o\) are those such that given \(N\), \(o\) maximises their possible returns. That is, for all possible \(P^\prime\), their payoff in \(\langle P, N \rangle\) is at least as large as their payoff in \(\langle P^\prime, N \rangle\).
  • The content players are those who are not happy, but who are such that for no strategy \(s^\prime\) which is an alternative to their current strategy \(s\), would they do better given what nature and the other players would do if they played \(s^\prime\).
  • The unhappy players are those who are neither happy nor content.

The standard Nash equilibrium condition is that no player is unhappy. So assume we have a Nash equilibrium  with only happy and content players. And assume it is also a Perfect Bayesian equilibrium. That is, for any ‘off-equilibrium’ outcome, each player would have some credence function such that their play maximises utility given that function.

Now add one constraint to those credence functions:

Intuitive Criterion - Weak Version

Assume a player has probability 1 that a strategy combination \(P\) will be played. Consider their credence function conditional on another player playing \(s^\prime\), wh­ich is different to the strategy \(s\) they play in \(P\). If it is consistent with everything else the player believes that \(s^\prime\) could be played by a player who is content with the actual outcome \(\langle P, N \rangle\), then give probability 1 to \(s^\prime\) being played by a content player.

That is, if some deviation from equilibrium happens, give probability 1 to it being one of the content players, not one of the happy players, who makes the deviation.

That’s enough to rule out the odd equilibrium for the baseball-hockey game. The only way that is a Perfect Bayesian equilibrium is if the barman responds to a hockey request by increasing the probability that the tourist is American. But in the odd equilibrium, the American is happy, and the Canadian is merely content. So the Intuitive Criterion says that the barman should give credence 1 to the hockey requester, i.e., the deviator from equilibrium, being Canadian. And if so, the barman won’t respond to a hockey request by guessing the requestor is American, so the Canadian would prefer to request hockey. And that means the outcome is no longer an equilibrium, since the Canadian is no longer even content with requesting baseball.

In games where everyone has only two options, the weak version I’ve given is equivalent to what Cho and Kreps offers. The official version is a little more complicated. First some terminology of mine, then their terminology next.

  • A player is happy to have played \(s\) rather than \(s^\prime\) if the payoff for \(s\) in \(o\) is greater than any possible outcome for \(s^\prime\) given \(N\).
  • A player is content to have played \(s\) rather than \(s^\prime\) if they are not happy to have played \(s\) rather than \(s^\prime\), but the payoff for \(s\) in \(o\) is as great as the payoff for \(s^\prime\) given \(N\) and the other players’ strategies.
  • A player is unhappy to have played \(s\) rather than \(s^\prime\) if they are neither happy nor content to have played \(s\) rather than \(s^\prime\).

If a player is happy to have played \(s\) rather than \(s^\prime\), and \(s\) is part of some equilibrium outcome \(o\), then Cho and Kreps say that \(s^\prime\) is an equilibrium-dominated strategy. The full version of the Intuitive Criterion is then:

Intuitive Criterion - Full Version

Assume a player has probability 1 that a strategy combination \(P\) will be played. Consider their credence function conditional on another player playing \(s^\prime\), wh­ich is different to the strategy \(s\) they play in \(P\). If it is consistent with everything else the player believes that \(s^\prime\) could be played by a player who is merely content to have played \(s\) rather than \(s^\prime\), then give probability 1 to \(s^\prime\) being played by someone who is content to have played \(s\), rather than someone who is happy to have played \(s\).

This refinement matters in games where players have three or more options. It might be that a player’s options are \(s_1, s_2\) and \(s_3\), and their type is \(t_1\) or \(t_2\). In the Nash equilibrium in question, they play \(s_1\). If they are type \(t_1\), they are happy to have played \(s_1\) rather than \(s_2\), but merely content to have played \(s_1\) rather than \(s_3\). If they are type \(t_2\), they are happy to have played \(s_1\) rather than \(s_3\), but merely content to have played \(s_1\) rather than \(s_2\). In my terminology above, both players are content rather than happy with the outcome. So the weak version of the Intuitive Criterion wouldn’t put any restrictions on what we can say about them conditional on them playing, say \(s_2\). But the full version does say something; it says other players should assign probability 1 to the player being type \(t_2\) conditional on them playing \(s_2\), since the alternative is that \(s_2\) is played by a player who is happy they played \(s_1\) rather than \(s_2\). Similarly, it says that conditional on the player playing \(s_3\), other players should assign probability 1 to their being of type \(t_1\), since the alternative is to assign positive probability to a player deviating to a strategy they are happy not to play.

The Intuitive Criterion has been the subject of an enormous literature. Goo­gle Scholar lists over 5000 citations for the Cho and Kreps paper alone, and similar rules are discussed in other papers. So there are arguments that it is too weak and arguments too strong, and refinements in all sorts of directions. But we’ll leave those. What I most wanted to stress was the form a refinement of Perfect Bayesian equilibrium would take. It is a constraint on the conditional credences of agents conditional on some probability 0 event happening. It’s interesting that there are some plausible, even intuitive constraints; in general it seems the economic literature has investigated rationality under 0 probability evidence more than philosophers have.

6.10 Spence and the Value of a Useless Signal

Let’s turn away from these very theoretical questions, and back to applications of these models to real world problems.

College Signal has an important history. It is a simplified version of the model Spence (1973) published as an account of the labor market, and which won him a share of a Nobel Prize. It triggered a huge debate, going to this day, about the economic significance of college. The debate is not about the mathematics of the model, which all check out. It’s about whether the model is an accurate representation of the real world.

Let’s look at those assumptions. In the separating equilibrium, high type students go to college and low type ones do not, employers hire college-goers and reject the rest, and everyone is behaving perfectly rationally. In the model, no one learns anything in college. A high type student is more valuable to employers, but they would have been just as valuable with no college. Indeed, them being hired without going to college would be a weak Pareto-improvement, since college is costly to the student. The student’s productivity is settled by their type, which Nature fixed before the game started. College does not change it. All college does is cost less to a high type student than to a low type one.

That asymmetry in cost is why the model works. Because the high type student finds college ‘cheaper’, and we’ll want to come back to what that means, there is a range of prices at which the high type student is willing to pay and the low type one is not, and paying it therefore reveals the type. The signal works because it is expensive, and because it is differentially expensive. It does not need to be useful.

Notice also how different this is from Simple Signal. There the players’ interests were aligned, and a costless signal did the job. You can get the separating equilibrium in a case where it doesn’t cost anything to leave one or the other signal.2 In this case, where the interests diverge, a costless signal couldn’t work. If college was costless, everyone would go. If it cost the same amount to everyone, it wouldn’t work.

So we might worry what this says about what we do here. Is college just a sorting mechanism? Does it produce a private gain but no social gain? Is it a massively expensive system that just makes us collectively worse off?

Now note that the model only gives us a consistency proof. We know, and we’ll go over this evidence more in Section 6.11, that college grads earn more than non grads. What Spence’s model shows is that this fact alone is consistent with college not actually teaching anyone anything. But lots of things are consistent without being all true. To do more, we need to look more at the details.

6.11 The College Wage Premium

There is a key fact that needs explaining. In almost every rich country, people who finish a degree earn substantially more over a lifetime than people who do not. According to recent OECD (2025) figures (see especially Table A4.1), on average across a range of countries, degree holders earn 40% more over their lifetime than people with high school degrees who have not completed college. In the United States, this premium is even more. Here, grads earn 64% more than people whose highest qualification is either a high school degree or a diploma. That’s a gap of well over a million dollars, probably closer to two million dollars. These are enormous numbers, and they need explaining.

There are three natural explanations which are worth distinguishing.

  • Human capital. College teaches things that are worth money. The premium measures the skills. This explanation doesn’t say where college teaches these skills; as far as this explanation goes it could be that you learn more from your fellow students in the dorms than in class.3 What’s central to this story is that people come out of college with more value to employers than they came in with.
  • Selection. Colleges admit people who were already going to do well. Just knowing that one group of students had higher SAT scores on average would suggest they’d earn more money. College grads do, on average, have much higher SAT scores than the general populace. Maybe that’s the whole story.
  • Signalling. College is a hurdle that is cheaper to clear for the people employers want. The premium is a consequence of this fact.

These are not exclusive, and the full explanation is likely to involve all three. But they are different, and a story where one of them explains 80% of the premium, and the other two explain 10% each leaves us with a very different picture than if a different one was the 80%.

Still, let’s see if we can make progress by thinking about what the world would look like if one of them were the whole story. Two of the explanations look fairly bad by that score.

If it were all human capital, then partial degrees should earn partial returns, and three years of a four-year degree should be worth roughly three quarters of the premium. If it were all selection, an admission letter should be worth almost as much as a diploma, since the letter is where the sorting happened.

Neither prediction holds. Two or three years of college buys very little, and the premium mostly arrives when the degree does. This is the sheepskin effect, named for the old practice of printing diplomas on sheepskin. This is the single strongest piece of evidence for the signalling story. It is hard to see why the fourth year of skill acquisition should be worth several times the third. It is easy to see why clearing a hurdle should be worth more than nearly clearing it.

The effect is easiest to see in the United States, which has an unusually large population of people with some college and no degree. In most countries that group is too small to say much about. In the American data there are millions of them, and their advantage over high school graduates is small. While it would definitely be nicer to have more data, we shouldn’t dismiss this American data: it does seem to tell against the purest version of the human capital story.

6.12 Does the Signalling Model Fit?

So that’s the first bit of evidence that the signalling theory is right. It predicts the sheepskin effect, the other two, if taken as comprehensive theories, predict it won’t exist. In fact, it does exist.

The model also explains why the different in wage premia between degrees is relatively small. So the model fits a world where a degree in medieval epistemology and a degree in accounting both open doors. We have to be careful, because there is some difference in premia difference between degrees, but overall I’d say there’s less than you’d expect on a pure capital model.4

The signalling model also explains something else about public universities like this one: the doors to the lecture theatres are unlocked. Anyone can walk in to any lecture.5 We are, on the whole, not overrun with people taking advantage of all these effectively free lectures. This is a little surprising on the human capital story.6

But there are also some difficulties.

The premium grows with age. Again, the Among workers in their late twenties the advantage is real but moderate; among workers in their late forties it is substantially larger. That feels like the wrong way round for a signal. The whole apparatus rests on employers being unable to see a worker’s type directly, and after twenty years of performance reviews, promotions and colleagues’ opinions, the type is not hidden any more. The signal should be losing value, and it is gaining. The key thing is that the signalling model turns on the presence or absence of a degree being a key part of how employers judge the quality of a would-be-employee, and that’s just not plausible after many years of work.

There are things the defender of the signalling model could say. The premium at forty-five may reflect twenty years of sorting inside firms rather than the degree itself, in which case the degree got someone through the first door and the rest is career. Or the wage structure may have been fixed early, by contracts or internal pay scales, and simply persist. Both are defensible, but we’re back in a situation where one theory is explaining things away, and the other theory is readily predicting them.

The cost assumption may not hold. The model needs college to be more costly for the low type than the high type, and it needs the correlation to be tight; this is the single condition that makes the separating equilibrium work at all. It is worth asking whether it is true.

The ‘cost’ here mostly isn’t literal dollar cost. Some people get more scholarships for academic success, and some people have to repeat courses they can’t complete. But none of these are making college literally two million dollars more expensive for low type students.

Rather, the point is that college is more pleasant for high types than low types. OK, who actually finds college fun, who pays the least psychic cost for being here? The students who flourish, who enjoy the reading and the seminars and are untroubled by exams, are not obviously the students who will be the most valuable employees. The traits that make academic life comfortable are not the traits that make someone reliable under a deadline, or good with clients, or sound in practical judgement. When I was a student, the students who were paying the least psychic cost were the perpetual student types, and I would be very hesitant of having hired them. More generally, one can be an excellent at many jobs that require college degrees while having found college genuinely painful, and one can be very happy indeed in a library and a poor fit for most workplaces.

This is a more serious objection than the first, because it attacks an assumption rather than a prediction. If there isn’t a correlation between the cost of degree and the value to the employer, the model breaks. Plenty of low types, from the perspective of employers, will sail through college because they love it here.

College isn’t expensive enough. The wage premium is huge. In the model, everyone is acting rationally. Some people ratioanlly turn down millions of dollars because the unpleasantness of those calculus classes is just too much. This beggars belief. In the modern university, with almost all courses being electives, not all courses will be that painful.

There is a technical point here. There is a variant on Spence’s model where the difference between high and low is not how much college costs, but whether it is an option at all. In the biological literature, signalling models often start with a costly signal that other animals cannot send. That’s still a signalling model, and a model where low types are simply incapable of finishing a degree, rather than able but unwilling, does not require thinking that there are all these people who will literally not take a calculus class even if they’ll be paid a million dollars for it.

Changes in degree length provide natural experiments. If the hurdle is what matters, then changing its height should not change the premium much, because employers will recalibrate and a three-year degree will come to mean what a four-year degree used to mean. If skills are what matter, cutting the coursework should cut the premium.

Some of the evidence favours the signalling reading. In some countries bachelor’s degrees take three years, and in other countries, like the US, they take four. On the whole, the premium doesn’t obviously reflect this length difference. Though the US, which has four year degrees, does have a larger than average premium. In the EU, the Bologna reforms shortened degrees across much of the continent without eliminating the graduate premium, though the master’s degree has become more expected since, which looks like the hurdle moving rather than disappearing.

But the cleanest social data we have points towards the human capital theory. Carolina Arteaga (2018) studied a reform at Universidad de los Andes in Colombia, which in 2006 cut the required coursework in economics by about 20% and in business by about 14%. The entering classes, their test scores and the graduation rates stayed put, and so did the university, the degree title and the diploma. The signal, in other words, was held fixed by construction. Earnings were not: graduates after the reform earned about 16% less in economics and about 13% less in business, and they did visibly worse in recruitment and ended up at weaker firms. Something in the missing courses was doing work that the diploma was not doing.

The reasonable reading is that both mechanisms operate. A degree is partly a hurdle and partly an education, and the proportions vary by institution and by labor market. What the model establishes is not that college teaches nothing. It is that a world in which college taught nothing could look, at least in broad outline, very much like ours.

6.13 Akerlof and the Market for Lemons

Spence (1973) shows what the informed side can do about asymmetric information. George Akerlof (1970) asks what happens when they cannot do anything about it, and the answer is that the market can break in unexpected ways.

For most of the twentieth century, the used car market had a striking feature. Of course the older a car is, and the more miles it has on it, the less value it has. And for most of the car’s life, this is a reasonably linear decline. There was, however, a big discontinuity. When you bought a car, and drove it off the lot, it immediately lost a big chunk of its value, maybe as much as 20%. Everyone knew this, but no one had a good theory for why it would be so.7

Akerlof suggested this worked because of asymmetric information. Let’s work with a really simple model. Most cars are good, but some are, in the terminology of the mid-twentieth century, lemons. They are just bad cars. You can drive them around the block on a test drive, but after a few days, weeks, or months at home, you’ll realise you bought a dud. This is private information; a buyer, even one who kicks the tires and drives it around the block a few times, can’t tell whether they are buying a lemon.

Let’s work with a really simplified model. Assume that sellers are motivated to sell; that’s to say, restrict attention to those people who have cars and would other things being equal like to sell them at market price. Assume that half the cars are lemons. That’s absurd, but it keeps the numbers simple. The values of good and bad cars are in Table 6.3.

Every car is worth more to a buyer than to its seller, so if everyone could see quality, every car would change hands, the market would clear, and everyone would be better off.

Now suppose buyers cannot tell the two apart. A buyer who thinks the odds are even values a random car at \(\frac{120 + 60}{2} = 90\), and so is willing to pay up to 90.

But a good car is worth 100 to its owner. At 90, no owner of a good car sells. So the only cars on the market are lemons, and the buyer who works this out revises: a car actually on offer is worth 60. Buyers now offer at most 60. Lemon owners, who value their cars at 40, are happy to sell.

The market clears, in the sense that the cars put up for sale sell, but only lemons trade, at 60. Good cars never change hands, even though every good-car sale would have produced a surplus of 20.

In more realistic models, where cars have continuous quality, there aren’t that many lemons, and some holders of good cars are really motivated to sell, you’ll get more trade. But still, the fact that a car is up for sale will be evidence that it’s bad, and the used-car discount is what buyers rationally demand in order to overcome this evidence.

The underlying structure here ends up being simmilar to the Spence model. Indeed, Spence was motivated to come up with his model after his advisors recommended reading Akerlof’s paper.8 The decision to put a car up for sale is a signal; a signal that you’re an unfortunate owner of a lemon.

As well as explaining the used-car discount that was prevalent for the twentieth century, Akerlof’s model also predicts the conditions under which the problem should ease. Those conditions have by now largely been met, and the discount is really small now. The model requires two things to hold. One is that there is enough variation in manufacturing quality that the possibility of lemons is a serious problem. That’s just not as big a deal now; most car companies have excellent quality control and the cars rolling off the line are pretty similar to one another. The other condition is that there is an information asymmetry between buyers and sellers. That isn’t nearly as dramatic now. Vehicle history services and cheap independent inspections, reduce the information gap, and for what’s left manufacturer certification removes the downside of buying a lemon. Both of the model’s assumptions were weakened, and the effect it was built to explain faded. That’s a really neat result; it’s one thing to explain a striking phenomenon, it’s another altogether to predict when the phenomena will fade.

One last very striking point for the philosophy students. Akerlof’s model was really unpopular with economists when it was first published. They thought he was abandoning rational choice explanation. This is odd because in his model, everyone is completely rational by the standards that philosophers would use. They all have sensible priors, which they update by conditionalisation, and then they utility maximise. What’s true is that in his model, not everyone is fully informed. Nowadays, I think economists have largely come around to philosophers’ way of talking; ignorance is unfortunate, but it’s not irrational. But there was something really sociologically puzzling about how economists reacted to it, and a different course would explore this more.
