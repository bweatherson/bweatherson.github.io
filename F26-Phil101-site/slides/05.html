How Many Sources of Knowledge?

Phil 101 · Day 5

Author

Brian Weatherson

Published

January 1, 2026

What feeds an inference

Where we are

Grue left us with a problem. A run of evidence, however clean, does not on its own fix what comes next. Inference needs something fed in from outside.

So the question for today is a plain one: what feeds it?

The game gave you a gift

In the deduction game the clues were handed to you, and they were true. That is the only reason you never had to guess.

. . .

Nothing outside the game works like that. No one hands you clues that are both given and guaranteed. So where do real clues come from?

Back to Holmes

We watched Holmes observe and infer. Look again at where his information actually comes from:

  • a telegram summons him to the case
  • the newspaper prints the inquest, and he reads the son’s statement on the train
  • Miss Turner comes to the inn and tells him about her father
  • Lestrade passes on what the police have found

. . .

His own eyes meet the evidence for the first time only at the pool. The rest he is working from because someone told him.

The third thing

Believing something because someone told you has a name. It is testimony.

It sits awkwardly beside the other two. You did not see it for yourself, and you did not work it out. You took someone’s word.

An old question

How many sources are there?

Long before Hume, philosophers in India asked just this and gave it a name. A pramāṇa is a source of knowledge, a way a true belief can be properly arrived at. The argument was over how many there are.

The ladder

  • the Cārvāka materialists allow one: perception
  • the Buddhist logicians allow two: perception and inference
  • the Nyāya school allows four: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony
  • later schools press on, reaching six

. . .

The four-source list adds two things the two-source list lacks: comparison, and the one that will occupy us, testimony.

A word on comparison

Comparison is the unfamiliar one. The stock example: you are told that a wild ox is much like a cow, you later meet the animal in the forest, and you know at once which kind it is.

That is the analogy from Day 3 put to a narrow job, fixing what a new word picks out by its likeness to something you already know.

The model you have been using

Our own tradition mostly settled on the two: perception and inference. You have spent the week living inside that picture.

The game was inference. Holmes was perception feeding inference. Neither left room for testimony as a source in its own right.

Is testimony its own source?

The question

Set perception and inference on one side. Does testimony belong beside them as a third source, or does it fold into them, so that you perceive the words and then infer that they are true?

We take that up in full next class. First, why it is worth the trouble.

Why it matters

Strip testimony out and look at what survives in what you know.

Nearly all of history, the sciences, and geography reached you on someone’s word, and so did most of what you treat as your own memory of early childhood. If testimony is not a way of knowing, most of your knowledge is not knowledge after all.

An old argument, an unlikely place

The dispute is not new. In 1927 an Indian philosopher, Dhirendra Mohan Datta, argued the case in Mind, putting the Nyāya side to his Cambridge contemporaries.

We read him next class. Today, get the lay of the land.

Where this goes

Next class, the question head on. Does testimony stand on its own feet? The reductionist says no, and we will see how hard that is to make stick.

. . .

Further out sit the questions it opens. Whom to trust when you cannot check for yourself. How to weigh experts who disagree. Why we lean on science. And the hardest report of all, that a miracle occurred.
