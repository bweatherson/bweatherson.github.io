Risky Inferences: Induction, Analogy, Explanation

Phil 101 · Day 3

Author

Brian Weatherson

Published

January 1, 2026

From safe to risky

Yesterday

In the deduction game, a verdict was allowed only when the clues forced it. The conclusion was already sitting inside what you were given.

. . .

That safety has a price. A valid deduction never hands you anything that wasn’t, in some form, already in the premises.

The cost of safety

To learn something new about the world, you have to take a risk. You have to draw a conclusion that runs past your evidence, and so might be wrong.

Inferences of that kind are the subject of today.

Three risky moves

We will look at three ways of reasoning past the evidence:

  • enumerative induction
  • argument from analogy
  • inference to the best explanation

By the end there is a case that the first two are versions of the third.

Enumerative induction

From some to all

You observe a run of cases and project the pattern onto the ones you have not seen.

Every raven anyone has recorded has been black, so the next raven will be black, and perhaps all of them are.

. . .

The premises can be true and the conclusion false. The inference is risky by design.

The leap is the point

Nothing in the black ravens you have seen forces a colour on the raven you have not. The move from seen to unseen is a leap.

We make it all the time, and mostly it pays. Why it pays is a question we press tomorrow.

Argument from analogy

From like to like

Two things are alike in several respects, so they are probably alike in one more.

A new drug cured the disease in mice. Mice and humans share the relevant biology, so the drug may cure it in humans.

. . .

The whole weight of the inference rests on whether the similarities are the ones that matter.

Inference to the best explanation

From clues to cause

You have some facts to account for. You ask which hypothesis, if it were true, would best explain them, and you conclude that it is true.

This is the detective’s move, so let us bring in the detective.

Holmes at the pool

In “The Boscombe Valley Mystery,” Holmes reaches the scene and changes:

Sherlock Holmes was transformed when he was hot upon such a scent as this… His nostrils seemed to dilate with a purely animal lust for the chase.

He crawls over the ground with a lens, reading the trampled grass.

What he calls it

Holmes calls his method deduction. It is no such thing.

From the stride, the boots, and a scrap of cigar ash, he settles on a tall, left-handed man who smokes Indian cigars. None of that is forced. It is the account that fits the marks better than any rival, and that is why he backs it.

. . .

That is inference to the best explanation, wearing a famous disguise.

Two jobs, not one

Watch Holmes work in two stages.

At the pool he gathers: the lens, the ash, the prints in the grass. That is perception.

Back at the inn he expounds, fitting the clues into one story. That is inference.

. . .

Holmes names the pair himself: “a trivial example of observation and inference. Therein lies my métier.”

Why the best, and not merely one

Holmes warns Watson off the easy reading:

There is nothing more deceptive than an obvious fact.

The work is not finding an explanation. It is finding the one that beats its rivals. He sets himself a rule for the case, to treat the son’s story as true and follow where that leads.

Pulling the three together

One move underneath

Harman’s suggestion is that the other two are inference to the best explanation in plainer clothes.

Take the ravens. Calling “all ravens are black” the best explanation of the run you have seen is what licenses the jump to the next one. The mice work the same way, with shared biology as the best explanation of the shared cure.

. . .

You need not accept this to use it. It is a way of seeing what the three have in common.

What they share

Each one reaches past the evidence to something the evidence does not force. That reach is what makes them useful, and what makes them fallible.

Holmes’s whole art lives in that gap, between the marks on the ground and the man who left them.

Where this leaves us

Every inference today began from something given: ravens already seen, results already in, marks already on the ground.

Tomorrow we ask what happens when even a spotless run of evidence fails to pin the answer down. That is the grue problem.
