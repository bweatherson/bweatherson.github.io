What is Welfare?

Phil 101 · Day 18

Brian Weatherson

2026-11-03

Where we are

What is a good outcome?

Sequentialism says that the right actions are those that produce the best outcomes. But this raises an obvious question:

  • What is the best outcome?

We will start with a question that seems relevant to this.

  • What is good for a human?

Welfare

When we talk about theories of welfare in a philosophy class, we mean to be talking about what makes human lives go well.

That’s what we’ll be doing for the next two weeks.

This question is ambiguous between two readings.

Two answers to ‘making’ questions

We need to distinguish between causal and constitutive answers to making questions.

This distinction is easiest to see with some examples. For instance, let’s think about the question, “What makes you win football games?”

Causal
The causal answers tell you what kinds of things bring about victories. For instance, practicing hard, or having a good game plan, might make you more likely to win a football game. In that sense, those are things that make you win football games.
Constitutive
Constitutive answers tell you what winning is. In football, it’s really easy. It’s having more points when the clock goes to zero.

We’re interested in constitutive answers to the What makes life go well question.

Causal answers

Also, answers are, of course, also interesting.

There is a lot of research, especially in psychology, about which things make your life go better. The most striking one in any studies I’ve seen is that quitting smoking is surprisingly good for your well-being on a whole lot of dimensions.

But we’re not going to be asking about how to make your life go better, as much as asking about what it is for life to be better.

Welfare and Morality

How does this connect back to consequentialism?

On one theory of what makes a world good, the connection is really close. The goodness of a world just is the sum of the goodness of the lives in it.

This raises three big questions that will be in the background for a bit:

  1. Which things have welfare? Do plants? Do kitchen appliances?
  2. Among those things, which of them matter to the goodness of a world? Which of them have moral standing?
  3. Is there anything to goodness beyond welfare, like beauty, or biodiversity?

Welfare and Morality

Of these questions, we’re going to mostly focus on question two.

One big issue is how much we should care about the interests, or welfare, of various animals and perhaps even of various non-living things.

But I think it will be easiest to discuss that once we’ve gotten clearer on what welfare is. Let’s start by thinking about human well-being.

The concept

Good, and good for

Lots of artworks, paintings, songs, movies are good. We can say quite a bit about why they are good.

But those qualities are not good for the artwork. Maybe careful preservation is good for a painting, but being beautiful, for instance, is not good for a painting, even if it is something that makes a painting good.

Welfare is the good for relation: how well a life goes for the one living it.

Not the same as being good

You can be a good person and have a miserable life. You can be a ratbag and have a wonderful one.

How often those combinations actually occur is a real question, and one that you see discussed extensively in several ancient traditions. But they’re clearly possible, and that’s enough to show that these must be separate questions.

Philosophers sometimes call welfare prudential value, to keep it clear of moral value and of aesthetic value.

It can go below zero

Welfare isn’t a measure of how much good stuff a life contains. It can be negative.

Someone in unrelieved pain, with no prospect of relief, has a life that is going badly for them. Arguably, or at least some people have argued, it’s worse than no life at all.

Our theory of welfare should allow for at least the logical possibility that some people’s lives have a negative value to them.

Fixing the question

We still haven’t said anything about what makes a life go well.

We’ve only said what we’re asking about: the things that are good for the person whose life it is.

The reason for doing things this way is that we want philosophers who have different theories of welfare to be talking about the same thing, even though they have different theories of it.

Identifying what the question is and what kind of thing might be an answer to it is one way to do that.

A moment, or a life

One last distinction. When we ask how someone’s life is going, there are two subtly different things we might be asking.

There’s how things are for that person right now, this morning, in this mood, in this room.

And there’s how their life is going, taken as a whole whole. Those can be different. A miserable afternoon in the middle of a good life is both a miserable afternoon and a good life.

A moment, or a life

Presumably, though, these two questions are related. It would be very strange to imagine someone having a wonderful life, every moment of which was horrible. Maybe that’s possible, but it would be strange.

One question to keep in mind when we look at theories of welfare is whether they start with well-being at a time and define the well-being of a life in terms of it, or, the other way around, they start with the well-being of a life and define well-being at a time in terms of it. Both options are definitely possible.

Three families

Three families of answer

Hedonism
Welfare is pleasure, and the absence of pain. That’s it.
Desire theories
Welfare is getting what you want. What it is you want doesn’t matter; what matters is that you got what you want.
Objective lists
Some things are good for you whether or not you want them, and whether or not they feel good.

Those are the next three classes. They aren’t the only options, but nearly everything else is a variation on one, or a combination of two.

Reclassifying

For some purposes, it’s helpful to divide the options like this.

Subjective
Something is good for you because of your attitude to it. You wanted it, or it felt good. The value to you is downstream of your attitude.
Objective
Some things are good for you whether or not you have any attitude toward them at all.

Hedonism and desire theories are both on the subjective side, though they focus on different attitudes. Objective lists are on the other side, though typically they’ll have pleasure and desire-satisfaction on the list.

A subjectivist argument

Many people think it seems wrong to say something is good for someone if it leaves them entirely cold. They get nothing from it, they never wanted it, and never would have.

That’s the core idea behind subjective theories, and it will be in the background of a lot of what we discuss.

iClicker POLL

Compare two lives of the same length.

In the first, someone is mildly content from beginning to end. Nothing much happens. Nothing goes wrong.

In the second, someone works at something hard, fails a great deal, and eventually completes the project they have been working on after considerable difficulty.

Which goes better for the person living it?

  1. The first
  2. The second
  3. Exactly as well as each other
  4. There’s no fact of the matter

Classifications

If you said A, you’re being pulled toward hedonism. The first life feels fine from end to end, and on hedonism that is all there is to it.

If you said B, you think something beyond felt contentment is making a difference. But notice that both of the other two families say that.

A desire theorist can say the second life is better because finishing the thing was what she wanted most, and she got it. An objective list theorist can say it is better because achievement belongs on the list, wanted or not.

What the poll can’t do

So that sorts you into hedonist or not. But we can look at other cases which do.

Someone is clever, healthy, and free to do anything at all. What she wants, more than anything, for her whole life, is to count the blades of grass on the lawns of Ann Arbor. She spends sixty years counting, and finishes.

The desire theorist has to say that life went very well. She wanted one thing enormously, and she got it. The objective list theorist says no: counting grass is not on the list, however badly you want it.

One more question, in the background

We keep saying a person’s welfare. The concept doesn’t obviously stop at people.

Different theories have different implications for what other things can have welfare.

On hedonist theories, only things that are conscious have welfare, because welfare is a matter of conscious feelings.

On preference satisfaction theories, only things that have preferences can have welfare. That might be a very different set of things than the things that are conscious.
