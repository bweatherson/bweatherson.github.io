Evidence and the Senses II: What Memory Keeps

Phil 101 · Day 6

Author

Brian Weatherson

Published

January 1, 2026

What the senses leave behind

Last time

We set up the evidentialist picture. Your beliefs answer to your evidence, and perception is the clearest case of first-hand evidence, the kind you check for yourself.

. . .

But perception is gone in a moment. What you actually carry around, and act on, is the residue: what you remember.

Memory keeps less than you’d think

A fact about memory, from the lab and from a second’s introspection alike: it stores contents, not sources.

You remember that the heart has four chambers. You do not remember where you learned it, from whom, or whether the source was any good.

. . .

It keeps even less than the content, but set that aside for today.

So you are carrying a lot of orphans

The result is a head full of beliefs whose origins you have lost. You know a great many things and could not say, for almost any of them, how you came to know them.

Most of the time that is fine. Sometimes it is the whole problem.

The forgotten source

A case you will recognize

Years ago you picked some claim up from a poor source: a half-read headline, a confident friend who turned out to be wrong, a guess you made under pressure.

You long ago forgot where it came from. Now you simply believe it, and it feels like something you know.

. . .

This is not a thought experiment. It is most of what sits in anyone’s head.

The puzzle

Put it as cleanly as we can. Almost all of your belief-forming methods are reliable; one of them is not. You formed the belief that p by the unreliable one, and then forgot that you had.

Today you have a memory that p, and nothing else.

Are you justified in believing p?

It pulls two ways

No. The belief came from a bad method and never picked up any new support along the way. Bad in, bad out.

Yes. Your memories are mostly of reliable things, so any given remembered belief is a fair bet, and the bad origin is gone. Forgetting laundered it.

The fork, without the jargon

The two answers grade the belief by different things. One looks back at where it came from. The other looks at how trustworthy your memory is in general.

Both have a real pull, and that is the puzzle. You do not need a theory of justification to feel it.

Why this one earns its place

It is how being wrong sticks

This is no curiosity. It is the mechanism behind a good deal of what we get wrong.

You meet a shaky claim, you discount it, you forget the source, you keep the claim. Months later it has shed its doubts and feels like plain fact. Psychologists call the rising pull of a discounted message, once its source falls away, the sleeper effect.

. . .

There is a blunter cousin. Sheer repetition alone makes a claim feel true, even one you know better than to believe. That is the illusory truth effect, and Lisa Fazio’s work on it is in the optional reading; we will come back to it.

. . .

A scrolling feed runs both at once: content in, source gone, and the same lines again tomorrow.

Memory and testimony are one puzzle

Where do the orphans come from?

Ask where forgotten-source beliefs actually start. Almost every one is forgotten testimony. Someone told you, or wrote it down; you kept the claim and lost the speaker.

. . .

So “may I trust a memory whose source I have lost” and “is testimony a way of knowing” are not two questions. Memory is where testimony goes to lose its source.

Which sets up next time

You trust your forgotten-source memories constantly, and you have no choice about it. But once you do, it is hard to turn to a living speaker and demand they prove their reliability before you will believe a word.

That is the pressure point for next time, when we ask whether testimony stands on its own.

Reading

Required:

“Epistemological Problems of Memory,” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, §2.4 (Preservationism and Generativism).

Recommended:

Lisa K. Fazio et al., “Knowledge Does Not Protect Against Illusory Truth.”

Loose end (optional)

Even deduction leans on this. Once a proof runs longer than you can hold in mind at once, you accept the conclusion because you remember checking each step, not because you are surveying the whole thing now.

Even the safest knowledge is not free of memory.
