Does Testimony Stand on Its Own?

Phil 101 · Day 7

Author

Brian Weatherson

Published

January 1, 2026

The question, again

Last time

We set perception and inference on one side and asked where testimony goes. Is it a third source, or does it fold into the other two?

. . .

Today we take the reducing view seriously, lean on it, and see what is left standing.

Reductionism

It is just perception and inference

The reductionist says testimony is not basic. Someone tells you the bridge is out. You hear the words, which is perception, and you conclude they are true, which is inference, because people of that sort, on roads of that sort, have been right before.

Your belief rests on perception and an inference from the speaker’s reliability. Testimony adds no new way of knowing.

Why hold it

The view is tidy. It keeps the list of sources short, and it makes trust earn its place. You believe a speaker because you have evidence that speakers like this are reliable, and not otherwise.

Hume reasons this way about reports of miracles, setting the testimony against everything the rest of experience tells us.

Two criticisms

The plan

Two objections do most of the damage. The first says the reductionist can never get started. The second says the standard it sets is too harsh, and would sink perception along with testimony.

First: you cannot get started

We do not actually infer

Watch yourself take in what people say. You believe it straight off, unless something trips an alarm. You do not stop to build an argument from the speaker’s track record.

Datta quotes Montague on the point:

Man is a suggestible animal and tends to believe what is said to him unless he has some positive reason for doubting the honesty or competence of his informant.

The well runs dry

Suppose belief did wait on an inference from past reliability. Where would the evidence come from?

You would need a stock of cases checked for yourself, with no one’s word taken. But almost everything you know about speakers, roads, and bridges arrived by testimony in the first place. The inference has nothing of its own to stand on.

Second: the standard is too harsh

Two questions, kept apart

Datta’s key move is to pull apart two things the reductionist runs together:

  • where the content of a belief comes from
  • how its truth gets checked

Testimony can be the source of the content even when the checking is done by some other route.

In his words

The attempt to reduce testimony to inference is based on a confusion between the source of a knowledge and the source of the knowledge of its validity.

You learn from a friend that he is glad. His words are the source of that fact. An inference might confirm that he is truthful, but it says nothing about his being glad. That came from him.

Turn it on perception

The reductionist demands that real knowledge be checkable by some other method. Datta turns the demand around.

If testimony ceases to be a source of knowledge because the truth of it has to be ascertained through inference, why should not even perception cease to be a source of knowledge?

Perceptions mislead too, and we check them against further perceptions. By the reductionist’s own rule, perception would reduce as well. The rule proves too much.

Where that leaves the debate

Anti-reductionism

Put the two criticisms together. We trust without inferring, and the demand that would disqualify testimony would disqualify the rest. So testimony looks like a source in its own right, level with perception and inference.

This is the Nyāya view Datta defends, and roughly the one much later writers worked their way back to.

But are we just dupes?

The gullibility worry

If we trust by default, we look like easy marks. A great deal of testimony is mistaken, and some of it is built to fool us. A theory that has us swallowing whatever we are told cannot be right either.

Epistemic vigilance

Trust, but filtered

Sperber, Mercier, and their colleagues in Paris offer a way through. We are neither blank trusters nor blanket doubters. We run a set of quiet checks as we listen:

  • on the source: is this person competent, and do they mean me well?
  • on the content: does this sit with what I already have good reason to believe?

Default trust is where we start, but it passes through a filter.

What the filter buys

This keeps both halves of the picture. We can learn from each other, which is the whole point of testimony, without lying at the mercy of every liar.

The trust is real, and so is the guard. That is how testimony can be basic without making us fools.

The machine version

A job we cannot yet hand off

Vigilance is so ordinary that we hardly notice doing it. Building it is another matter.

A system that takes in testimony at scale, trained on whatever it is fed, has no steady grip on whether a source can be trusted or whether a claim hangs together. The check a child runs without trying is something we do not know how to engineer.

. . .

It is the shape from the grue week once more. The hard part was never the storing. It is the prior judgment about what to trust.

Where we land

Testimony stands on its own feet, but vigilant feet. You trust, and you watch.
