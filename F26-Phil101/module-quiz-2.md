## Day 10 — Russell, "Appearance and Reality"

**Q1.** ★★ What does Russell's argument in this chapter actually run on?

A. Optical illusions, in which perception misleads us about how things are.
B. Variation in how the table appears from different positions and in different light, where nobody is being deceived at all.
C. Dreams and hallucinations.
D. Scientific discoveries about the nature of matter.

*Answer: B.* The most useful question on Russell, and the one most students will get wrong, because the tidied-up version they will meet elsewhere is called the argument from *illusion*. Russell isn't claiming anyone is fooled by the table's apparent angles. He is pointing out that the appearances disagree with each other and that no appearance has a better claim than the rest.

**Q2.** ★ Russell concludes that the table has no one real colour. His reason is that

A. colour is a purely subjective matter.
B. the colours it shows under other conditions have just as good a claim to be real, so singling one out would be favouritism.
C. science has shown that colour is really wavelength.
D. in the dark the table has no colour at all, so it never had one.

*Answer: B.* Notice the shape of this. It is not that any particular appearance is wrong. It is that they all have equal standing, and equal standing plus disagreement means none of them is the real one.

**Q3.** On shape, Russell's claim is that

A. the table has no shape.
B. the real shape is not what we see, but something inferred from what we see.
C. the table's shape varies with the observer.
D. shape, unlike colour, is genuinely in the object and directly perceived.

*Answer: B.* D is the position a lot of people expect Russell to take, since shape feels more objective than colour. He does not take it — from almost anywhere you stand, a rectangular table presents two acute angles and two obtuse.

**Q4.** ★ Russell introduces two terms. Which pairing is right?

A. *Sense-data* are the experiences of being aware; *sensation* is what those experiences are of.
B. *Sense-data* are the things immediately known in sensation — colours, sounds, smells, hardnesses, roughnesses; *sensation* is the experience of being immediately aware of them.
C. *Sense-data* and *sensation* are two words for the same thing.
D. *Sense-data* are physical objects; *sensation* is the mental image of them.

*Answer: B.* A is the pairing reversed, and it is the single most common slip with this vocabulary. The distinction matters for the rest of the chapter: the sense-datum is the object, the sensation is the awareness of it.

**Q5.** Once the table and what you see have come apart, Russell says two questions arise. Which two?

A. Is there a real table at all? And if so, what sort of object can it be?
B. Do other people see the same table? And can we ever know?
C. Is the table's colour real? And is its shape real?
D. What is a sense-datum? And how do we know about them?

*Answer: A.*

**Q6.** Having dealt with colour, Russell runs the same move through the table's texture under a microscope, its shape from different points of view, how hard it feels under different pressures, and the sound it makes when rapped. Why work through all of them rather than resting on the colour case?

A. To make the chapter long enough to stand alone.
B. To show that the problem is general across the senses, rather than a quirk of vision that a theory of colour might dissolve.
C. Because the colour case is the weakest of the five.
D. Because each sense requires a different kind of sense-datum.

*Answer: B.* A structural question rather than a content one, and it separates reading from skimming. It also forecloses the natural escape route, which is to treat colour as a special case.

*Corrected after checking the text.* The stem originally gave the order as colour, shape, texture, touch, sound. The chapter runs colour, texture, shape, touch, sound, and the sound gets a single clause rather than a paragraph.

**Q7.** Russell mentions Berkeley and Leibniz as philosophers who accepted that something is there but denied it is matter in the ordinary sense. How do their answers differ?

A. They don't; the two views are the same.
B. Berkeley holds that what is there is mental — ideas, and minds to have them. Leibniz holds that what we call matter is a colony of souls.
C. Berkeley denies anything is there at all; Leibniz says it is matter.
D. Berkeley says it is matter; Leibniz says it is ideas.

*Answer: B.*

**Q8.** ★ What does Russell take the value of the whole exercise to be?

A. Proving that the external world does not exist.
B. Showing that ordinary certainties are not as simple as they look, and that strangeness and wonder lie just below the surface of the commonest things.
C. Establishing that science is more reliable than common sense.
D. Demonstrating that perception is never a source of knowledge.

*Answer: B.* A and D are both stronger than anything Russell claims in this chapter, and both are what students report him as saying when they have taken the conclusion from the lecture rather than the chapter.

## Day 11 — Nagel, and the colour swatches

**Q9.** ★★ In the swatch poll, people disagreed about which patch was pure green. Three explanations were offered. Which one can a lab rule out, and which can it not?

A. It can rule out that people mean different things by "yellowish"; it cannot rule out that their eyes differ.
B. It can rule out differences in lighting and viewing angle; it cannot settle whether the remaining disagreement is about the words or about the experiences.
C. It can rule out all three.
D. It can rule out none of them.

*Answer: B.* The whole point of running the demonstration before Nagel. Once the lab has removed the boring explanation, what is left is a human-scale version of the bat problem — and it is left standing, not solved.

**Q10.** Settings for pure green vary across people with ordinary colour vision by about 55 nm. Why is that figure striking?

A. Because for any one person, the gap between pure green and pure blue is not much more than 55 nm.
B. Because 55 nm is the width of the whole visible spectrum.
C. Because it is smaller than the variation for other colours.
D. Because it shows most people are colour-blind.

*Answer: A.* Without that comparison the number means nothing; with it, the variation is as wide as the distance between two different colour names.

**Q11.** ★ Nagel's statement is that an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to be that organism. What does the second half of his sentence add?

A. Nothing; it is a restatement.
B. That it is something it is like *for the organism* — from the inside, not to an observer.
C. That the organism can report on it.
D. That other organisms can imagine it.

*Answer: B.* The clause doing all the work, and the one students drop when they paraphrase.

**Q12.** A car with a fuel gauge has representations of its own states. On Nagel's account, is it conscious?

A. Yes — self-representation is what consciousness amounts to.
B. No. Nagel explicitly sets self-awareness in that sense aside, along with the ability to make self-reports and intelligence.
C. Yes, provided the gauge is accurate.
D. Nagel does not say.

*Answer: B.*

**Q13.** Nagel's point about bat sonar is that

A. it is more accurate than human vision.
B. it is clearly a form of perception, yet is not similar in operation to any sense we possess, and there is no reason to think it is subjectively like anything we can experience or imagine.
C. it works only in darkness.
D. bats are not themselves conscious of it.

*Answer: B.*

**Q14.** ★ In most inquiry, becoming more objective takes you closer to how things really are. Nagel's claim about consciousness is that

A. the same holds; we just need better instruments.
B. a shift to greater objectivity takes us farther from the phenomenon rather than nearer, because its subjective character is comprehensible only from one point of view.
C. objectivity is impossible.
D. consciousness is not a real phenomenon.

*Answer: B.* The move that makes the paper more than a good example.

**Q15.** Physics replaces seen colour with wavelength, and felt warmth with mean kinetic energy. The difficulty is that

A. these replacements are mistaken.
B. each replacement is a genuine gain, and each one drops exactly the thing we are trying to explain when we study consciousness.
C. physics cannot measure anything subjective.
D. the replacements are circular.

*Answer: B.* A is the answer of someone who has heard "problem for physics" and stopped listening. Nagel thinks the replacements are successes.

**Q16.** ★★ Which of these does Nagel actually claim?

A. Physicalism is false.
B. It would be a mistake to conclude that physicalism must be false; the trouble is that we have no conception of how it might be true.
C. Physicalism is true but unprovable.
D. The mind-body problem is a pseudo-problem.

*Answer: B.* The most-misreported sentence in the paper, including by people who like the conclusion.

## Day 12 — Chalmers and Jackson

**Q17.** Which of these is an *easy* problem in Chalmers's sense?

A. Why any of the brain's processing should be accompanied by experience at all.
B. How the brain focuses attention.
C. Why we are not zombies.
D. Whether there is something it is like to be a bat.

*Answer: B.* "Easy" is relative — these are enormous research programmes. What makes them easy is that we know what an answer would look like.

**Q18.** ★ What are the labels "easy" and "hard" for?

A. Ranking problems by how much funding they deserve.
B. Spotting when someone has answered one problem and presented it as an answer to the other — explaining how the brain tells red light from green light, and offering that as an explanation of colour experience.
C. Showing that the hard problem is insoluble.
D. Distinguishing psychology from neuroscience.

*Answer: B.*

**Q19.** In Jackson's argument, everything rests on the second premise. What holds that premise up?

A. Experimental evidence about colour vision.
B. The sense that of course Mary learns something when she comes out.
C. The definition of physicalism.
D. Chalmers's zombie argument.

*Answer: B.* The first premise is true by stipulation, so the whole argument stands on one intuition. Noticing how much weight sits there is the point.

**Q20.** ★ Someone knows that John is an unmarried man without recognising that John is a bachelor. In lecture this was an analogy for which response to the knowledge argument?

A. The ability hypothesis.
B. Phenomenal concepts — Mary learns no new truth, but comes to grasp an old one in a new way.
C. The denial that Mary learns anything.
D. Jackson's own view.

*Answer: B.* And the open question the lecture left you with is what plays the role of "bachelor" here.

**Q21.** Which response says that what Mary gains is not knowledge of a fact at all?

A. She learns a new fact.
B. The ability hypothesis.
C. Phenomenal concepts.
D. She learns nothing at all.

*Answer: B.*

**Q22.** ★ Jackson replies to the ability hypothesis with a test case. What is it?

A. Mary tries to ride a bicycle.
B. Mary comes out and wonders whether other people's experience of red is like hers — a question about how things are, not about what she can now do.
C. Mary describes the wavelength of red light.
D. Mary forgets what she learned inside the room.

*Answer: B.*

**Q23.** ★ What exactly is claimed about philosophical zombies?

A. That they exist.
B. That nobody thinks they exist, but that they might have — and if they might have, the physical facts do not settle all the facts.
C. That some people are zombies without knowing it.
D. That zombies lack intelligence.

*Answer: B.* A and C are what the word suggests; neither is the argument.

**Q24.** Jackson later changed his mind about the knowledge argument. The worry flagged in lecture was that his earlier view made experiences

A. impossible to describe.
B. central to our lived reality and at the same time causally inert.
C. identical to brain states.
D. available only to colour scientists.

*Answer: B.*

## Day 13 — the case for dualism

**Q25.** ★ Why did vitalism come up at the start of the lecture?

A. As an example of a view that turned out to be true.
B. As a non-religious parallel: a view on which the physics of inorganic matter was insufficient to explain how living things worked.
C. Because Descartes was a vitalist.
D. Because vitalism is still the mainstream view in biochemistry.

*Answer: B.* The point being that dualism does not require a soul in any religious sense — you can want an extra ingredient without wanting a spiritual one.

**Q26.** ★ The lecture ran this parody: I know the Phil 101 lecturer is Brian; I don't know the Phil 101 lecturer is the only Australian on the philosophy faculty; so Brian is not the only Australian on the faculty. What does it show?

A. That the premises must be false.
B. That knowing something under one description and not under another tells you about descriptions, not about how many things there are.
C. That the conclusion is true.
D. That arguments about identity are always invalid.

*Answer: B.* This is the objection to the flying man and to Descartes's real distinction, met first on a case where the failure is obvious.

**Q27.** In Descartes's real distinction argument, which line carries the weight?

A. Line 1, that I understand my mind as a thinking, unextended thing.
B. Line 3, that clear and distinct perception can be trusted.
C. Line 4, the step from what I can clearly and distinctly understand to what can be separated.
D. Line 5, the conclusion.

*Answer: C.*

**Q28.** ★★ Descartes appeals to God to get from conceivability to possibility. Why doesn't that obviously settle it?

A. Because God's existence has not been established at that point in the *Meditations*.
B. Because we would need the further claim that it is no part of *God's* understanding of my mind that it is not extended.
C. Because God cannot separate substances.
D. Because the appeal is circular.

*Answer: B.* The hardest question in this set, and the one that rewards having followed the lecture rather than a summary. A is false — God's existence and non-deception are established before Meditation VI.

**Q29.** Descartes denies that you are in your body as a sailor is in a ship. What is the point?

A. That you cannot steer your body.
B. That the union is tighter than that: a sailor *observes* that her ship is damaged, while you *feel* that your body is damaged.
C. That the body is more important than the mind.
D. That ships are not conscious.

*Answer: B.* And it is the thing that costs him on Thursday — the tighter the union, the harder it is to explain.

**Q30.** If the arguments of Nagel, Jackson and Chalmers work, what do they get you?

A. Substance dualism, and a soul that can exist without the body.
B. Property dualism — something about experience isn't captured physically — but not a separable soul.
C. Physicalism.
D. Nothing at all.

*Answer: B.* For a separable soul you need the flying man or the real distinction, which are the two arguments the lecture spent its time poking holes in.

**Q31.** ★ Descartes puts everything on the diagonal of the grid: whatever thinks also feels, and whatever feels also thinks. Why is he entitled to that?

A. It is an empirical observation about animals.
B. Because if thought and consciousness are both modes of one mental substance, whatever has either has both — so the diagonal follows from *substance* dualism in particular.
C. Because the off-diagonal cells are incoherent.
D. He is not entitled to it; he simply assumes it.

*Answer: B.* The consequence worth seeing: give up the substance and you have given up the reason for the diagonal, which is why people who reject his ontology shouldn't help themselves to his conclusion.

**Q32.** On the grid, which cell does a philosophical zombie occupy?

A. Thinks, and is conscious.
B. Thinks, but is not conscious.
C. Is conscious, but does not think.
D. Neither thinks nor is conscious.

*Answer: B.*

## Day 14 — the case against dualism

**Q33.** ★ What makes Elisabeth's objection hard for Descartes to brush off?

A. She denies that souls exist.
B. She grants him everything — that there are souls, that the soul thinks, that it is unextended — and then asks how it does the one thing he agrees it does, using his own physics to pose the question.
C. She appeals to experimental results he had not seen.
D. She argues that God would not allow interaction.

*Answer: B.*

**Q34.** Elisabeth says movement depends on how much a thing is pushed, the manner of the push, or the surface and shape of the pusher. What does she draw from that list?

A. That all three require contact.
B. That the first two require contact and the third requires the causally active thing to be extended — and the soul has neither contact nor extension available.
C. That motion is impossible.
D. That the soul must be made of a very fine matter.

*Answer: B.*

**Q35.** ★★ "I would find it easier to concede matter and extension to the soul than to concede that an immaterial thing could move and be moved by a body." What method is on display?

A. Proof by contradiction.
B. Weighing costs: the question is not whether a view is obviously right, but whether it is less costly than the alternatives.
C. An appeal to authority.
D. Inference to the best explanation.

*Answer: B.* The lecture's point that this is more standard now than it was then is worth remembering — it is also what the essay in November will ask you to do.

**Q36.** Descartes's reply is that there are three primitive notions — extension for body, thought for soul, and their union — and that knowledge consists in keeping them apart. What does that amount to as an answer?

A. That the union is basic, so asking how it works in terms of extension applies the wrong notion.
B. That the soul is extended after all.
C. That the pineal gland does the work.
D. That interaction is an illusion.

*Answer: A.* Whether that is an answer or a renaming of the problem is what the poll asked you.

**Q37.** ★ In June 1643 Descartes concedes that the human mind cannot clearly conceive the soul's distinctness from the body and its union with the body at the same time. Why does that concession cost him so much?

A. It contradicts his physics.
B. His argument for dualism ran on what can be clearly and distinctly conceived, so he is conceding that the thing he needs cannot be clearly conceived.
C. It commits him to physicalism.
D. It was never published.

*Answer: B.*

**Q38.** The modern descendant of Elisabeth's worry is the claim that physics is causally closed. What does that mean?

A. That physics explains everything.
B. That every physical event which has a cause has a sufficient physical cause — so a non-physical mind is either among the physical causes or is doing nothing.
C. That the physical world is deterministic.
D. That minds cannot exist.

*Answer: B.*

**Q39.** ★ The identity theorist compares mind-brain identity to lightning being an electrical discharge. What is the comparison for?

A. To show that both are dangerous.
B. To show that identities like this are discovered scientifically, not by thinking about the words or imagining what is possible — so the fact that we can conceive of mind without brain proves nothing.
C. To show that lightning is mental.
D. To show that identity claims are always necessary truths.

*Answer: B.*

**Q40.** Functionalism came out of the lecture with an uneven record. What was the unevenness?

A. It works for animals but not for humans.
B. It is a fairly plausible account of what it is for a state to be a *belief*, and much less plausible for *pain*, since a pain has a functional role but also feels a certain way.
C. It works for pain but not for belief.
D. It answers the hard problem but not the easy ones.

*Answer: B.* And this is why property dualism keeps its foothold: you can be a functionalist about thought and still think consciousness is left over.

## Day 10 — one more, found while checking the text

**Q41.** ★★ Under a microscope the table shows roughnesses invisible to the naked eye. Russell asks which is the 'real' table, and his answer is that

A. the microscope shows it, since instruments are more reliable than eyes.
B. neither does, because a still more powerful microscope would change the answer again — so if we cannot trust the naked eye, we have no reason to trust the microscope either.
C. the naked eye shows it, since that is how tables are actually used.
D. the question is meaningless.

*Answer: B.* Worth adding because it is a different move from the colour argument. There the appearances have equal standing and none can be privileged; here each correction is itself correctable, so the regress never bottoms out. A is where most people's instinct goes, and Russell names that instinct — "We are naturally tempted to say that what we see through the microscope is more real" — before refusing it.
