# Short Assignment 2 — marking sheet

For the GSI meeting. Student-facing handout is `short-assignment-two.qmd`.

Worth 10%. Marked out of 20: **3 / 5 / 5 / 7** across the four questions, which
puts the weight where the thinking is. Marking window 23 October to 6 November.
Scripts must go back on 6 November — the essay prompt rides out with them, and
slipping that date eats the essay window rather than the marking window.

---

## What each question is testing

**Q1, state the view (3 marks).** Accuracy and economy. They should be able to
say what is being claimed without defending it. The commonest failure is
starting the argument here — a Q1 that already contains "because" is usually
borrowing from Q2. Do not deduct for that; mark Q1 on whether the claim is right
and move on. A student who states it in sixty clean words has done better than
one who states it in two hundred.

**Q2, the strongest argument (5 marks).** Full marks need the argument set out
so that a disagreeing reader can see where to push — premises identifiable, and
the step doing the work visible. Reporting that "Jackson argues physicalism is
false" is a restatement of Q1, not an argument.

**Q3, the most serious objection (5 marks).** Marked on whether they picked a
real objection and stated it at its strongest. A strawman costs marks even when
it is accurately reported, because the question says *most serious*.

**Q4, reply and verdict (7 marks).** Two halves. The reply, which is reportage
and should be the defender's best move rather than a weak one; and the verdict,
which is theirs. **Both verdicts earn full marks.** What is marked is whether a
reason appears, and whether the reason engages the reply they just gave rather
than restating Q3. Say this to the GSIs explicitly: first-time graders read "say
whether it succeeds" as concealing a right answer, and mark down students who
disagree with the lecture.

## Option A — the knowledge argument

**Q1.** Physicalism is false; there are truths about experience that complete
physical information leaves out. Mary is the case meant to show it, not the view
itself. A script that describes the black-and-white room and stops has answered
the wrong question.

**Q2.** Jackson's own three lines, or a faithful version: Mary knows everything
physical about other people; she does not know everything about them, since she
learns something on release; so some truths escape the physical story. Full
marks should notice that the first premise is true by stipulation and the whole
weight sits on the second.

**Q3.** The three in play are the ability hypothesis (Lewis and Nemirow — she
gains know-how, not a fact), the phenomenal concepts strategy (a new way of
grasping a fact she already knew), and the flat denial that she learns anything
(Dennett and the Churchlands). Any of the three, well stated, is full marks. The
phenomenal concepts version is the hardest to state correctly and the most
impressive when it is.

**Q4.** Against the ability hypothesis, Jackson's reply is that what matters is
not the kind or manner of Mary's knowledge but what she knows, with the test case
of Mary wondering whether other people's experience of red is like hers — a
question about how things are, not about what she can do. Against phenomenal
concepts, the reply is that the view owes an account of what such a concept is in
physical terms, when the defining feature is that you can only get one by having
the experience.

*Known wrong turn:* treating Jackson's later change of mind as settling it. That
he abandoned the argument is a fact about Jackson, not an objection, and a script
that leans on it has dodged the question.

## Option B — naive realism

**Q1.** You are directly aware of the object; looking-some-way is a relation
between the thing, the light and you. The contrast is with sense-data, and a
script that only says "the opposite of Russell" has not stated a view.

**Q2.** The case is largely negative, and that is legitimate here: sense-data put
a veil between you and the world, so every ordinary belief becomes an inference
from private patches; they make shared evidence impossible, since nobody can
check another's sense-data, which turns every joint observation into testimony;
and they are strange objects, with nothing physical bearing the relevant shade.
Full marks for any two of those pressed properly. A script that adds Russell's
own concession — that colour depends on the table, the spectator, and the way the
light falls — has found the strongest card in the deck.

**Q3.** The argument from illusion, and the Phenomenal Principle in particular:
if something looks darker, then there is something darker you are aware of. Full
marks need premise 2 identified as the load-bearing one.

**Q4.** The reply is to deny the Phenomenal Principle: nothing is darker, the
square merely looks darker, and looks are relations rather than objects — no more
needing a private bearer than my being north of the table needs a private
direction. Good scripts will then press on hallucination, where there is no
object at all for the relation to hold of. That is the standard pressure point
and a script that finds it unaided is doing well.

*Known wrong turn:* arguing that naive realism is right because illusions are
rare. The checkershadow is not a malfunction and Russell's own case runs on
ordinary variation, not deception.

## Bands

| | |
|---|---|
| **Full** | Does what the question asks, accurate, and the reasoning is on the page. |
| **Most** | Right, but a step is compressed or left implicit. |
| **Half** | Broadly right with one real error, or half of a two-part question missing. |
| **Some** | On topic, shows some grasp, answers a question other than the one asked. |
| **Little** | On topic and not much more. |
| **None** | Blank, or unrelated. |

Grade generously; this is a calibration sample and the first piece of extended
writing most of them have been read on. Expect most scripts between 13 and 16
out of 20. Do not deduct for spelling, grammar, or absent citations — the
handout tells them citations are not needed.

## Before marking starts

- Each GSI marks the same twenty scripts; compare the three sets before anyone
  marks the rest. Two of the three have not marked before, and this is 10% of
  the grade.
- Read by question, not by student — all the Q1s, then all the Q2s. It builds
  the scale out of the actual answers instead of an imagined ideal, and it keeps
  the two options comparable.
- Check the option split early. If one option has attracted five scripts and the
  other a hundred and forty, that is worth knowing before the essay prompt is
  written, and it says something about which of lectures 10 and 12 landed.
