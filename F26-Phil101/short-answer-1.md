# Short Answer 1 — draft prompts and marking scheme

**Written by hand in section, week of 5 October. Thirty minutes. Worth 8%,
inside the section component.**

Eight candidate prompts below; four are marked ★ as a suggested set. Then a
marking scheme and three worked answers, which are the part the GSIs will
actually need.

---

## Two format decisions, and why

**Closed book, with anything needed printed on the sheet.** This is the only
sample of unassisted prose the course collects before the essay, and that is
what it is for. Open notes turns it into a test of whose notes are better and
adds a source of variance that has nothing to do with the thing being measured.
The cost is that no prompt may depend on recalling a definition, so any
definition a prompt needs is printed with it. Prompt 3 is the case in point.

**Suggested times printed next to each prompt.** Students who budget badly
produce a strong first answer and three fragments, which is noise rather than
signal, and novice graders read that pattern inconsistently. Printing "about 7
minutes" costs nothing and removes some of it.

**Four prompts cannot cover nine lectures.** Trying to sample the whole range
gets you four shallow prompts. The ★ set instead runs along one spine — when is
a belief any good, and what happens when the belief came from someone else —
covering Days 5 to 9. If you would rather have the induction half represented,
swap in Prompt 3 for Prompt 6.

## The prompts

**Prompt 1 ★** *(about 6 minutes)*

> Explain, in your own words, the difference between an **epistemic** reason to
> believe something and a **practical** reason to believe it.
>
> Then give one example of each. Do not use an example from lecture or from the
> readings.

*Day 5. Tests a distinction plus transfer. The examples are where the grading
signal is: a student who has the distinction produces two clean cases quickly,
and a student who doesn't produces two versions of the same case.*

**Prompt 2** *(about 6 minutes)*

> Here is an argument:
>
> > Every student in this room has a student ID. Anyone with a student ID is
> > enrolled at Michigan. So everyone in this room is enrolled at Michigan.
>
> Is it valid? Is it sound? Explain your answers. If you think one of these
> questions cannot be settled from the information given, say which and why.

*Day 2. The last sentence is the whole question: it's valid, and whether it's
sound turns on whether the premises are true, which you can't tell from here
(there may be a visitor in the room). Do not use this one alongside Prompt 1;
both reward the same skill of holding two notions apart.*

**Prompt 3** *(about 8 minutes)*

> Call something **grue** if it is examined before 1 January 2030 and is green,
> or is not examined before then and is blue.
>
> Every emerald anyone has examined has been green. Every emerald anyone has
> examined has also been grue.
>
> Explain why this is a problem for the rule "if every *F* we have examined has
> been *G*, expect the next *F* to be *G*."

*Day 4. The definition is printed so this isn't a memory test. Students who
understood the lecture will get to "the same evidence supports two
incompatible predictions"; students who didn't will restate the definition.*

**Prompt 4 ★** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Jo believes that the Battle of Hastings was in 1066. She is right. She has
> believed it since she was small, and she cannot remember where she learned it
> or why she first believed it.
>
> Give the best reason for saying Jo's belief is justified, and the best reason
> for saying it is not. Then say which you find more convincing, and why.

*Day 6. The three-move shape — both sides, then a verdict — is exactly what
Short Answer 2 and the essay ask for, so this is the prompt that does the most
work for what comes later. It is also the one most likely to produce a real
disagreement between two good answers, which is worth telling students.*

**Prompt 5 ★** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Two people disagree about testimony. One says you should believe what a
> stranger tells you only if you have some positive reason to think they are
> reliable. The other says you should believe them unless you have some
> positive reason to doubt them.
>
> Describe a situation in which these two give different advice. Then explain
> why the disagreement between them is narrower than it first looks.

*Day 7. The second half is the real question, and the one students consistently
miss: the two views agree about every case where you have some reason one way
or the other, and differ only where you have none.*

**Prompt 6 ★** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Anderson argues that a person who cannot assess a scientific claim can still
> assess the scientists making it.
>
> Explain why she thinks the second task is easier than the first. Then give
> one reason to doubt that it is as easy as she suggests.

*Day 8. The second half will separate the class. The available doubts include
the circularity worry about who counts as a crank, the fact that a dishonest
expert can be well credentialled, and the fact that judging "responsiveness"
requires knowing what a real response would look like.*

**Prompt 7 ★** *(about 8 minutes)*

> Sam only ever encounters arguments for one side of a political question. He
> has never met a serious argument for the other side, and it has never occurred
> to him that there is one.
>
> Mia has met the arguments for the other side many times. She was told, long
> before she met them, that people who make those arguments are paid to make
> them, and she has found this to be true of several of them.
>
> Someone hands each of them a well-argued piece from the other side. Say what
> you expect to happen in each case, and why the two cases come apart.

*Day 9. Two named cases and a "why" is a more reliable prompt than asking for
the definitions, because it can't be answered by writing out two definitions.
Students who only have the labels will produce the labels and stop; the ones
who have the mechanism will say something about what Mia does with the article.*

**Prompt 8** *(about 6 minutes)*

> On the two-part model of a learning system, perception takes things in from
> the world and processing does something with what came in.
>
> Explain how the existence of optical illusions was offered as evidence for
> keeping these two parts separate.

*Day 3. Included for coverage, but it is the weakest of the eight: it asks for
one argument to be reproduced and gives the student nothing to do with it.
Use it only if you want the first week represented.*

## Marking scheme

Each prompt out of **5**. Total out of **20**, scaled to 8%.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5** | Answers the question asked, is accurate, and the reasoning is visible on the page. |
| **4** | Answers the question and is accurate, but a step is compressed or left implicit. |
| **3** | Broadly right, with one real error, or with one half of a two-part question missing. |
| **2** | On topic and shows some grasp, but does not answer the question that was asked. |
| **1** | On topic, and not much more. |
| **0** | Blank, or unrelated to the prompt. |

**Grade generously, and here is what that means concretely.** Most answers
should land on 3 or 4. A 5 should be reachable by a student who did the reading
and thought for seven minutes — it is not a distinction grade, and if a section
is producing almost none, that section is marking harder than the others.
Reserve 0 and 1 for blanks and non-answers. A median total of 14 or 15 out of 20
is about right.

**Do not deduct for:** spelling, grammar, handwriting, crossings-out,
paragraphing, or failing to use technical vocabulary. A student who explains the
epistemic/practical distinction without once using either word has done the task.
These are thirty-minute handwritten answers and the point is to see how they
think, not how they proofread.

**Do not deduct for an unoriginal example.** Prompt 1 asks for examples not from
class. An example that is a thin relabelling of a class example — the doctor
becomes a dentist — still earns the mark if the point is right. The instruction
is there to stop pure reproduction, not to grade creativity.

**Do reward:** the student showing why, not just what. Two answers can reach the
same verdict and differ by a mark, and the difference is whether the page shows
the reasoning or only the result.

## Worked examples, Prompt 1

Give these to the GSIs before they start, not after.

**A 5.**

> An epistemic reason is a reason that makes the belief more likely to be true.
> A practical reason is a reason that makes me better off for holding it, whether
> or not it's true. The second kind doesn't bear on whether it's so.
>
> Epistemic: my downstairs neighbour's bike is gone from the rack, which is a
> reason to think he's out, because it's the kind of thing that happens when he
> goes out.
>
> Practical: believing my team will win makes the game more fun to watch. That's
> a reason to hold the belief but it's not a reason to think they'll win.

*Both notions stated, and the second example carries its own explanation of why
it is the kind it is. That last clause is what a 5 looks like.*

**A 3.**

> Epistemic reasons are about evidence and practical reasons are about what's
> useful. Epistemic: I see dark clouds so I think it will rain. Practical: I
> believe I'll do well on the exam so I feel better.

*Right on both counts, and both examples are correct. It loses marks because
"about what's useful" leaves out the part that matters — that a practical reason
doesn't bear on truth — and because neither example is explained. This is what
most of the pile will look like.*

**A 2.**

> An epistemic reason is a good reason to believe something and a practical
> reason is a bad one. For example, believing something because you want it to
> be true is a practical reason, which is not a good reason.

*On topic and not confused, but it hasn't answered the question. It gives a
verdict on the two kinds rather than the difference between them, and there is
only one example where two were asked for. Note that it isn't wrong, which is
why it isn't a 1.*

## Notes for the GSI meeting

**Read by prompt, not by student.** All of Prompt 1 across the pile, then all
of Prompt 2. It is the standard trick and it matters more with first-time
graders, because it builds the scale out of the actual answers instead of out
of an imagined ideal.

**Mark twenty, then compare.** Each GSI marks the same twenty scripts — or,
cheaper, the same five — and the three sets get compared before anybody marks
the rest. Twenty per cent of the grade is being assessed by three people, two of
whom have never done it. One hour spent here is worth more than any amount of
rubric wording.

**Check the distributions around week eight**, as planned, but also check them
the day after this is marked. The gap between sections will never be easier to
close than immediately after the first batch.

## Still to settle

- **Makeup path.** Expect ten to fifteen absences across six sections. One
  scheduled slot the following week, decided before the date is announced, is
  cheaper than arranging them individually.
- **Accommodations.** Extended time and keyboard requirements cannot be handled
  inside a 50-minute section without arrangement, and at 150 students there will
  be several. This needs checking before the date is fixed, not after.
- **Whether students are told the prompts' topics in advance.** Telling them
  "four prompts drawn from Days 1 to 9" is enough, and is what the syllabus
  implies. Telling them which lectures would turn thirty minutes of thinking
  into thirty minutes of recall.
