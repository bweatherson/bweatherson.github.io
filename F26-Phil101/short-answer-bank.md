# Short answer prompt bank

Prompts by lecture, for use in Short Answer 1, in section, or in the written
part of the final. Format assumptions are the ones from `short-answer-1.md`:
handwritten, closed book, one short paragraph each, with any definition a prompt
needs printed alongside it.

Each prompt carries a suggested time, what it is testing, what a full-credit
answer contains, and the way it most often goes wrong. The last two are the part
the GSIs need, and they are what makes a prompt gradeable by three people
consistently.

---

## A scope problem worth settling first

**Russell is Lecture 10, and Short Answer 1 is advertised as covering Days 1 to
9.** He will have been taught by the week of 5 October, so the prompts below are
answerable. But there is a better reason than the advertised scope to keep him
out of it: **Short Answer 2 is set on Days 10 to 12**, and goes out on 8 October.
Using Russell in both means a student can write the same material twice, three
days apart, and the second piece stops being a test of anything.

So the recommendation is to leave Short Answer 1 at Days 1 to 9, which means the
Nguyen prompts are live for it and the Russell prompts are not. The Russell
prompts still earn their keep in three other places: section discussion in week
6, a practice piece handed out with Short Answer 2 as a worked warm-up, and the
short-answer section of the final.

## Day 6 — Frise

**F-a. Two things called preservationism** *(about 8 minutes)*

> Two different claims both go by the name "preservationism":
>
> - that memory cannot create justification which was not there already; and
> - that a belief which enters memory justified keeps that justification.
>
> Explain how somebody could accept the second of these and reject the first.
> Describe a case in which the difference between them shows up.

*Tests:* the distinction the reading is most careful about and students are
likeliest to run together.
*A full answer:* holds that memories which are fully preserved keep whatever
status they arrived with, while allowing that a belief arriving without
justification can pick up the justification an ordinary memory belief has. The
case is the forgotten source: a belief that entered badly, lost its history, and
now sits in a generally reliable memory.
*Common failure:* restating both theses accurately without ever building the
position that separates them. The prompt asks for a combination, not a contrast.

**F-b. What the word list showed** *(about 8 minutes)*

> In class, a list of words was read out, and a large number of people afterwards
> reported having heard a word that was never said.
>
> Explain what this is supposed to show about how memory works, and why it
> matters for the question of whether memory can generate justification.

*Tests:* whether they see that the demonstration attacks a *motivation* rather
than a thesis, which is a distinction worth having before the essay.
*A full answer:* memory is reconstructive rather than merely reproductive — it
builds something plausible from patterns and later prompts, which a storehouse
does not do. The relevance is that the storehouse picture was the main reason for
thinking memory could only preserve. Losing the picture does not refute
preservationism; it removes its support.
*Common failure:* concluding straight from the demonstration that memory is
unreliable, and stopping. That is a claim about accuracy, and the question was
about justification.

**F-c. Dependence, twice** *(about 8 minutes)*

> Udayana argued that memory is not a separate source of knowledge because it is
> dependent: whatever you know by memory had to get into you some other way
> first.
>
> Explain why this argument, if it works, makes trouble for counting testimony
> as a separate source too. Then say whether you think that is a problem for the
> argument.

*Tests:* Day 6 against Day 7, and it is the best synthesis prompt in the bank.
*A full answer:* the transfer argument against testimony has the same shape —
you only learn from me if I knew already, so testimony passes knowledge along
rather than producing it. On the second half, the interesting move is that
Datta's reply carries back: what matters is not how knowledge gets made in the
abstract but whether *this person* comes to know something, and by that test
both memory and testimony do fine. A student who reaches that has done well.
*Common failure:* noticing the parallel and treating it as a point in the
argument's favour rather than as a cost, which is the natural reading if you
think fewer sources is tidier.
*Use as a stretch prompt.* It is the hardest thing in this file, and it will
separate the top of the class rather than the middle.

## Day 7 — Datta

*These are keyed to `slides/07.qmd`, which was written from the PDF but has not
been re-checked against it this term. None of them turns on Datta's exact
wording, so the risk is low, but the arguments are worth a glance before use.*

**D-a. Equal standards** *(about 8 minutes)*

> A reductionist says that testimony is not a source of knowledge in its own
> right, because you always have to check whether the speaker is reliable before
> you are entitled to believe them.
>
> Explain Datta's reply, and say what the reply is meant to show about the
> reductionist's principle.

*Tests:* the argument Datta is actually best at, and a reductio, which they met
in week one.
*A full answer:* perception gets checked too — you walk closer, or you look
again, or you ask somebody who was nearer. So if needing to be checked
disqualifies a source, perception goes as well, and everybody agrees perception
stays. The principle therefore proves too much and should be given up.
*Common failure:* answering with Datta's earlier and weaker observation, that we
do not in fact check most of what we are told. That is a different argument, and
Datta himself concedes it can be absorbed.

**D-b. Naming the source** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Datta accepts that what looks like a single piece of knowledge is usually a
> complex process, with different parts of it coming from different sources.
>
> Explain why he does not think this shows that testimony is really something
> else.

*Tests:* a concession-then-reply structure, which is the move the essay will ask
for in November.
*A full answer:* we already name the source by the last step everywhere else.
Seeing a table involves memory supplying the concept and perception supplying
this one here, and we call the whole thing perception because the new part came
through the eyes. Testimony should be treated the same way.
*Common failure:* reproducing the concession and never getting to the reply, so
the answer ends up arguing against Datta by accident.

**D-c. Whether vigilance helps or hurts** *(about 8 minutes)*

> Here is an argument on Datta's side. We cannot check everything we are told.
> We are only required to do what we can. So we cannot be required to check
> everything we are told.
>
> Explain how the idea of epistemic vigilance is supposed to answer this
> argument, and say whether you think it succeeds.

*Tests:* whether they have the direction of the move right, and this is the
whole point of including it.
*A full answer:* vigilance denies the first premise. If the initial check is
cheap enough — a fast pass that tolerates false alarms and reserves real scrutiny
for whatever it flags — then we can run something on everything, and the
argument loses its footing.
*Common failure:* treating vigilance as support for Datta because it came later
in the lecture. It cuts the other way, and a student who gets the direction
backwards should be marked as having missed the question rather than as having a
minority view.

## Day 9 — Nguyen

**N-a. The remedy mismatch** *(about 8 minutes)*

> A university decides to address political division on campus by requiring
> every student to take a course that presents both sides of several contested
> issues.
>
> Explain why Nguyen would expect this to work for some students and not for
> others, and say what distinguishes the two groups.

*Tests:* the bubble/chamber distinction, and the thing the distinction is for.
*A full answer:* says that the course supplies missing voices, which is exactly
what a bubble lacks and not at all what a chamber lacks; a chamber member has
already been given a reason to discount those voices, so hearing them again may
confirm rather than disturb.
*Common failure:* defining both terms accurately and never returning to the
course. The prompt asks what happens to the students, not what the words mean.

**N-b. Why the warnings work** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Part of what makes an echo chamber hard to leave is that members are told in
> advance what outsiders will say, and why they will say it.
>
> Explain why this technique works better when the warning is true than when it
> is false.

*Tests:* the subtlest point in the essay, and one the lecture makes explicitly.
*A full answer:* a false warning can be checked and found false, and then the
source loses credit. A true one — some academics really are threatened, some
researchers really do hold equity — survives checking, and every confirmation of
it is also a confirmation of the source who issued it.
*Common failure:* answering that the warning works because members are gullible.
That is the reading of the essay Nguyen spends several paragraphs refusing.

**N-c. Thinking for yourself** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Someone says: "The way out of an echo chamber is to stop relying on other
> people and think for yourself."
>
> Give Nguyen's reason for rejecting this, then say whether you find the
> rejection convincing.

*Tests:* the point students most need and least expect, plus a verdict.
*A full answer:* Nguyen holds that this sort of intellectual self-sufficiency is
not available to anyone, since almost everything any of us knows rests on
trusting people we cannot check. The verdict half is open; both directions can
earn full marks.
*Common failure:* treating the rejection as pessimism about *these particular*
people rather than a claim about everybody, including the person giving the
advice.

**N-d. Vigilance turned around** *(about 8 minutes)*

> Earlier in the course we met the idea that we protect ourselves against bad
> testimony by running quick, cheap checks on what we are told.
>
> Explain how an echo chamber can turn that protection into a liability.

*Tests:* Day 7 against Day 9, which is the kind of connection the essay is worth
teaching for.
*A full answer:* the checks are checks on the source. A chamber works by
supplying the answers those checks return, so running them conscientiously
delivers the chamber's verdict. The more diligent the member, the more confirmed
she is.
*Common failure:* describing vigilance and describing the chamber without
joining them. Award the mark for the joint, not the two halves.

**N-e. Two halves of one picture** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Nguyen says that leaving an echo chamber takes rebuilding your whole network
> of trust, and also that this is not something a member can set in motion by
> herself.
>
> Explain why these two claims belong together.

*Tests:* the closing move, and whether they read to the end.
*A full answer:* what needs rebuilding is trust, and the decision to start
rebuilding would itself have to come from somewhere the member already trusts —
which is the chamber. So the first move has to be made from outside, by somebody
who earns trust before any argument gets made.
*Common failure:* reading the second claim as mere gloom rather than as a
consequence of the first. This is the hardest of the five; use it as a stretch
prompt rather than a core one.

## Day 10 — Russell

**R-a. Not an illusion** *(about 8 minutes)*

> Russell's argument in "Appearance and Reality" does not depend on anyone being
> deceived about the table.
>
> Explain what it depends on instead, and say whether that makes the argument
> stronger or weaker than one built on illusions.

*Tests:* the single most common misreading of the chapter.
*A full answer:* it runs on variation — the table shows different colours and
shapes from different positions, and nobody is fooled by any of them. On
strength, the good answer is that it is stronger, because an opponent cannot
dismiss it as a rare malfunction: the variation is present in every ordinary
case of seeing anything. A well-argued "weaker" can also earn full marks.
*Common failure:* writing about the checkershadow. That is the lecture's
example, not Russell's, and it is an illusion, which is the thing the prompt is
asking them to notice he does not need.

**R-b. Avoiding favouritism** *(about 8 minutes)*

> The table looks one colour from where you are standing and a different colour
> from where I am standing.
>
> Explain, in your own words, how Russell gets from that to the claim that the
> table has no one real colour. Then give the best objection you can think of.

*Tests:* an argument reconstruction plus a push back, which is the shape of the
essay they will write in November.
*A full answer:* no appearance has a better claim to be the real one than any
other, so picking one would be arbitrary; and if none of them is it, the table
does not have one. Objections worth full credit include privileging standard
viewing conditions, and denying that "the real colour" has to be one of the
appearances at all.
*Common failure:* reporting the conclusion without the equal-standing step,
which is the only place the argument does any work.

**R-c. Whose evidence** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Suppose Russell is right that what you are immediately aware of is always
> sense-data, and that your sense-data are yours alone.
>
> Explain one problem this creates for the idea that two people looking at the
> same table share any evidence.

*Tests:* Day 10 against the testimony weeks, and it is the consequence of the
view that students find most unsettling once they see it.
*A full answer:* each person is aware only of their own patches, so what passes
between them is not evidence but reports about private objects — which turns
every apparently shared observation into testimony, and leaves no way to check
one person's sense-data against another's.
*Common failure:* stopping at "everyone sees things differently", which is a
much weaker claim and one nobody disputes.

**R-d. Shape and colour** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Many people are willing to say that colour is not really in the object, but
> resist saying the same about shape.
>
> Explain how Russell treats shape, and say whether you think the two cases are
> different.

*Tests:* whether they read past the colour paragraphs.
*A full answer:* he treats shape the same way — from almost any position a
rectangular table presents two acute and two obtuse angles, so the real shape is
inferred rather than seen. On whether the cases differ, either verdict can earn
full marks; what is being graded is whether a reason is given.
*Common failure:* asserting that shape is objective because it can be measured,
without noticing that measuring is one more appearance among others on Russell's
account.

**R-e. The second question** *(about 7 minutes)*

> Russell says two questions arise once the table and what you see have come
> apart: whether there is a real table at all, and, if so, what sort of thing it
> could be.
>
> Explain why answering yes to the first question does not dispose of the
> second.

*Tests:* whether they noticed Berkeley and Leibniz, and what they were doing in
the chapter.
*A full answer:* both of those figures answer yes — something is there — and
still disagree entirely about what it is, one saying it is mental and the other
that it is a colony of souls. So "there is a table" is compatible with wildly
different accounts of what a table is, and the second question survives.
*Common failure:* treating the second question as a request for more detail
about a physical object, which misses that the interesting answers deny it is a
physical object.

## Notes for use

**Do not run N-a and the Sam/Mia prompt in `short-answer-1.md` together.** They
test the same thing, and N-a tests it better because it cannot be answered by
writing out two definitions.

**N-d and R-c both reward connecting two weeks of the course.** One of them is
worth including wherever these get used. They are also the two most likely to
produce an answer better than the one the prompt anticipated, which is worth
telling the GSIs so they do not mark to a template.

**Every prompt here that ends "and say whether you think" has an open second
half.** Both verdicts can earn full marks, and what is graded is whether a
reason appears. Say this to the GSIs explicitly. First-time graders reliably
assume there is a right answer hiding behind that phrasing, and mark down the
students who disagree with the lecture.

**F-a and D-c are the two to use if you only take one from each.** F-a is the
distinction Frise himself flags as easy to miss, and D-c is the only prompt in
the bank where getting the direction of an argument backwards is the expected
error rather than an unexpected one.

**Do not run F-c alongside the Jo/Hastings prompt in `short-answer-1.md`.** Both
are Day 6, and Jo is the gentler of the two; running them together spends two
prompts on one lecture.
