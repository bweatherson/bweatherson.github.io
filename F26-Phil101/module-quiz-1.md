# Module Quiz 1 — draft question bank

**Covers:** Lectures 1–9. **Opens:** after class Tue 29 Sep. **Due:** Sun 4 Oct.
**Format:** open book, multiple choice, on Canvas. Worth 2.5%.

Fifty questions below, grouped by lecture. Seventeen are marked ★ as a
suggested core set: one or two per lecture, weighted towards the questions that
need a distinction applied rather than a fact retrieved.

Each question carries the answer and, where it matters, a note on what a
particular wrong answer tells you. Those notes are the only thing here that a
self-grading quiz can give you that the grade itself can't.

## Day 1 — Introduction

**Q1.** ★ According to the course's three rules, which of the following is true?

A. You may not get help from friends or family on written work.
B. Any help you get — from people, books, or machines — should be acknowledged as precisely as possible.
C. AI use is acceptable so long as it isn't mentioned.
D. The rules in this course are the same as the rules in your other courses.

*Answer: B.* D is the distractor worth watching: the lecture said explicitly not to assume the norms carry across, and a student who picks D is the one who will be surprised later.

**Q2.** The course is organized around three questions. Which set?

A. How do we learn about the world? What kinds of things are minds? What do we owe each other?
B. Does God exist? Is there free will? What is the meaning of life?
C. What is truth? What is beauty? What is justice?
D. What is philosophy? What is science? What is art?

*Answer: A.*

**Q3.** Midgley compares philosophy to plumbing. The point of the comparison is that both deal with a system that

A. is dirty and unpleasant to work on.
B. is best left to licensed professionals.
C. lies beneath the surface, is usually unnoticed, was never consciously planned as a whole, and matters a great deal when it goes wrong.
D. can be replaced entirely when it fails.

*Answer: C.*

## Day 2 — Arguments

**Q4.** ★ Consider: "All cats are verbs. President Grasso is a cat. So President Grasso is a verb." This argument is

A. valid but unsound.
B. invalid, because its premises are false.
C. sound.
D. invalid, but with a true conclusion.

*Answer: A.* B is the distractor that earns its place. Students arrive thinking "valid" means "good," and they will keep thinking it unless something forces the issue.

**Q5.** ★ "Either the switch is on or it is off. If it is on, the wiring is faulty. If it is off, the wiring is faulty. So the wiring is faulty." Which move is this?

A. Reductio ad absurdum
B. Argument by cases
C. Enumerative induction
D. Inference to the best explanation

*Answer: B.*

**Q6.** "Suppose Amir is guilty. Then, by the first clue, Dana, Evan and Fay are all innocent. But then the second clue needs three criminals from Gil and Ira alone, which is impossible. So Amir is innocent." Which move is this?

A. Argument by cases
B. Reductio ad absurdum
C. Enumerative induction
D. Deduction by form alone

*Answer: B.*

**Q7.** "You never need to guess" was unpacked as three promises. Which of the following was **not** one of them?

A. Everything you are told is true.
B. At every stage you can establish some further person's status with certainty.
C. If you keep making such moves you will eventually fill the board.
D. Every clue is needed at some point in the solution.

*Answer: D.*

**Q8.** An argument, as we are using the term, has

A. exactly one premise and exactly one conclusion.
B. at least two premises and exactly one conclusion.
C. any number of premises and exactly one conclusion.
D. any number of premises and any number of conclusions.

*Answer: C.*

## Day 3 — Models of learning

**Q9.** ★ Why were optical illusions offered as evidence *for* the two-part model of learning?

A. They show that perception is unreliable, so some other faculty must correct it.
B. Perception goes on delivering a result that conflicts with what you already know, which is what you would expect if the perceptual system ran on its own logic rather than on your beliefs.
C. They show that processing is fallible.
D. They show the line between perception and processing is not sharp.

*Answer: B.* A is very tempting and gets the argument backwards: unreliability on its own shows nothing about the architecture. What does the work is that knowing the answer doesn't make the illusion go away. This is the single question here I would most want to see the response distribution for.

**Q10.** Two features of deductive reasoning were said to matter for automation. Which pair?

A. It is certain, and it is fast.
B. It chains without degrading, and its validity can be checked from the form of the argument alone.
C. It is language-dependent, and it requires understanding.
D. It is fallible, and it is hard to automate.

*Answer: B.*

**Q11.** "Every blade of grass I have ever seen has been green, so the next one will be green" and "the only sensible explanation of these boot-prints is that the man limped" are, respectively,

A. inference to the best explanation and enumerative induction.
B. enumerative induction and inference to the best explanation.
C. both enumerative induction.
D. both deductive.

*Answer: B.*

**Q12.** ★ The cognitive penetration thesis claims that

A. perception is unreliable.
B. what you believe, expect or want can shape what you actually see, not merely what you conclude from what you see.
C. your reasoning can direct where you point your eyes.
D. some of what you know is innate.

*Answer: B.* C is the sharpest distractor on this quiz. It is the qualification made *before* cognitive penetration was introduced — you think about where to look, and then your eyes just see. A student who picks C has read the slide and missed the distinction it was drawn to set up.

**Q13.** The xkcd comic about the national park and the bird was used to illustrate that

A. programmers exaggerate how long things take.
B. two requests can sound equally reasonable to the person asking while one is a database lookup and the other a multi-year research programme — and in 2014 that split tracked the split between safe and risky processing.
C. machine learning was already solved by 2014.
D. image recognition is a deductive problem.

*Answer: B.*

## Day 4 — Grue

**Q14.** ★ An emerald is dug up and examined today, and is found to be green. Is it grue?

A. Yes.
B. No.
C. Only if it is still green after 10 December 2026.
D. The predicate doesn't apply to emeralds that have already been examined.

*Answer: A.* Examined before the date and green is exactly what grue requires. If a substantial number miss this, they have not parsed the definition, and nothing downstream in the lecture will have landed.

**Q15.** An emerald first examined on Christmas Day 2026 turns out to be grue. What colour is it?

A. Green
B. Blue
C. Either, depending on when it was mined
D. Neither

*Answer: B.*

**Q16.** A student objects: "Grue is a cheat, because it has a date built into it and green doesn't." Goodman's reply is that

A. dates are perfectly acceptable in inductive predicates anyway.
B. if you take grue and bleen as your starting vocabulary, then *green* has to be defined using a date — so which predicate "has a date in it" depends on which language you started from.
C. green is more fundamental than grue, so the asymmetry is real.
D. nobody actually speaks that way.

*Answer: B.* D is Goodman's own answer to the overall riddle, but it is not his reply to *this* objection, and the two get run together constantly.

**Q17.** ★ What does the grue puzzle show about building machines that learn from examples?

A. Machines cannot perform inductive reasoning at all.
B. Which generalization a system draws is not settled by the data. It comes from how the system was built, which makes it a design decision rather than a discovery.
C. A large enough training set settles which predicate to project.
D. Deductive systems face exactly the same problem.

*Answer: B.* D is wrong for a reason worth knowing: deductive validity survives translation, so a deduction machine doesn't need its language chosen for it in the same way.

**Q18.** Goodman's own answer to the riddle appeals to

A. the fundamental physics of colour.
B. the fact that 'green' is entrenched in our practices, including our linguistic practices, and 'grue' is not.
C. a proof that green-hypotheses are more probable.
D. the claim that grue is meaningless.

*Answer: B.*

## Day 5 — Epistemic foundations

**Q19.** ★ A student asks her advisor whether there is any reason to think she will pass the exam. The advisor says: "Yes — students who expect to pass tend to perform better." The advisor has offered

A. an epistemic reason.
B. a practical reason.
C. both.
D. neither.

*Answer: B.* Same shape as Arpaly's doctor. The advisor has changed the subject, and the student is entitled to be annoyed.

**Q20.** The objection that a mind's coherence is a *global* property — so it would grade all of your beliefs alike, when in fact some of them are sensible and some are silly — is an objection to

A. infinitism.
B. coherentism.
C. foundationalism.
D. fallibilism.

*Answer: B.*

**Q21.** Modest foundationalism differs from classical foundationalism in that it

A. denies that there are any basic beliefs.
B. allows basic beliefs to be fallible, corrigible or dubitable.
C. requires basic beliefs to be infallible.
D. allows the chain of reasons to run on forever.

*Answer: B.*

**Q22.** ★ Faced with the fact that perception can mislead, one move is to say that what is really basic is not *square A is darker* but *square A looks darker to me*. One of the two problems raised for that move was:

A. Looks-claims are always false.
B. It is the wrong kind of thing to build on — you now need some reason to get from facts about your own mind out to facts about the world, and the attempts to supply one have not gone well.
C. It commits you to coherentism.
D. It requires you to have infinitely many beliefs.

*Answer: B.*

**Q23.** The schools of classical South Asian philosophy were often distinguished from one another by their disagreements about

A. whether knowledge is possible at all.
B. how many pramāṇa there are, and which ones.
C. whether perception is reliable.
D. whether testimony is ever honest.

*Answer: B.*

**Q24.** If a school declined to list some method as a pramāṇa, that could mean either that they distrusted the method, or that

A. the method was too new.
B. they thought it worked, but was only an application of methods already on the list.
C. the method was only available to experts.
D. no one had yet named it.

*Answer: B.*

## Day 6 — Memory

**Q25.** Udayana's reason for not counting memory as a separate source of knowledge is that memory is

A. unreliable.
B. dependent: whatever you now know "by memory" had to get in by some other means first.
C. not a form of perception.
D. shared with animals.

*Answer: B.*

**Q26.** The Jaina case — you saw the mug on the table yesterday, this morning you rummage through cupboards, then you recall that it was on the table — is supposed to show that

A. memory is reliable.
B. something was learned by recollection, which is trouble for the view that memory can only hand on what was already there.
C. memory works like a library.
D. perception is more basic than memory.

*Answer: B.*

**Q27.** ★ Frise's two theses can come apart. Which combination is coherent?

A. Anti-generativism is true and the historical thesis is false.
B. The historical thesis is true and anti-generativism is false: beliefs that enter memory justified keep that justification, *and* memory can also confer justification that wasn't there before.
C. Both must stand or fall together.
D. Neither can be held without coherentism.

*Answer: B.* This is the one students will get wrong by treating "preservationism" as a single claim.

**Q28.** In the word-list demonstration, a large share of the room reported having heard a word that was never read out. That result tells most directly against

A. generativism.
B. the storehouse or library model of memory.
C. foundationalism.
D. fallibilism.

*Answer: B.*

**Q29.** ★ In the forgotten-source case, one verdict runs: "The belief is justified. Memory is a reliable thing in general, and a belief you find in there is a fair bet." That verdict grades the belief by

A. where the belief originally came from.
B. how trustworthy memory itself is.
C. how well it coheres with the rest of what you believe.
D. whether it can be doubted.

*Answer: B.*

## Day 7 — Testimony

**Q30.** On the reductionist view, believing what you are told consists of

A. perception plus inference.
B. a distinct basic source of knowledge.
C. memory plus inference.
D. an inference from the speaker's sincerity alone.

*Answer: A.*

**Q31.** ★ Reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimony agree about a good deal. Where they actually disagree is over cases in which

A. you have good reason to believe the speaker.
B. you have good reason to doubt the speaker.
C. you have no particular reason either way.
D. the speaker is an expert.

*Answer: C.* Everything else in the lecture depends on seeing this, and it is the thing most often lost.

**Q32.** ★ Datta's equal-standards argument against the reductionist works by showing that

A. testimony is more reliable than perception.
B. the reductionist's own principle, applied evenly, would disqualify perception as a source too — which everyone agrees is absurd, so the principle must be no good.
C. people do not in fact check what they are told.
D. perception depends on testimony.

*Answer: B.* Its shape is the reductio from the logic games: reject the assumption because it causes a crisis elsewhere.

**Q33.** Lackey's creationist biology teacher is a counterexample aimed at which claim?

A. That the students learn by testimony.
B. That you gain knowledge from testimony only if the speaker already knew.
C. That testimony can generate knowledge for a particular person.
D. That perception is a basic source.

*Answer: B.*

**Q34.** Datta's first reply to the argument that testimony can only *transfer* knowledge is that

A. knowledge can be created out of nothing.
B. the question is not how knowledge in the abstract gets generated, but how *I* come to know something — and that can happen even if the transfer picture is right.
C. speakers always know what they assert.
D. testimony is more reliable than memory.

*Answer: B.*

**Q35.** The vigilance response of Sperber and Mercier is a reply to the premise that

A. we do not in fact check what we are told.
B. we could not possibly check everything we are told, so checking cannot be required of us.
C. testimony is a basic source.
D. people are gullible.

*Answer: B.* The reply is that the first-pass check can be cheap enough to run on everything, tolerating false alarms so long as it catches what matters, with expensive checking reserved for whatever it flags.

## Day 8 — Whom to trust

**Q36.** ★ Anderson proposes that laypeople judge *whom* to believe rather than *what* to believe. What makes the substituted question more tractable?

A. Character is harder to fake than data.
B. The things it asks you to check — credentials, funding, retractions, whether the person answers objections — are matters of public record that you can assess without understanding the science.
C. Scientists are generally honest.
D. The expert consensus is always correct.

*Answer: B.*

**Q37.** ★ A sceptic is shown the whale fossil sequence and replies, word for word, "There are no examples of transitional fossils between one species and another." A second sceptic instead replies, "*Ambulocetus* and *Rodhocetus* cannot be transitional, because they are reptiles." Which is true?

A. Both replies display dialogic irrationality.
B. Only the first displays it; the second is almost certainly false, but it at least has the form of a response, which is what the charge is about.
C. Only the second displays it, because it is false.
D. Neither displays it, since neither reply is from an expert.

*Answer: B.* The whole point of the test is that you can apply it without knowing any palaeontology, and that it therefore won't catch everything.

**Q38.** A researcher declines to release her data, published the result in a journal she edits, and is funded by the firm that sells the treatment. On Anderson's scheme, this is evidence against

A. her expertise only.
B. her honesty only.
C. her epistemic responsibility only.
D. more than one of the three.

*Answer: D.* The funding goes to honesty; withholding data goes to epistemic responsibility; nothing here bears on expertise. (This is close to the Day 8 clicker question — worth varying the details if you want a fresh read rather than a recall of the room's answer.)

**Q39.** Anderson holds that on one matter ordinary citizens are as well placed as scientists. Which?

A. Assessing statistical methods
B. Making the value judgments that scientific work embodies
C. Evaluating experimental design
D. Judging degrees of expertise

*Answer: B.*

**Q40.** Anderson's advice about consensus is that

A. you should suspend judgment before a consensus of trustworthy experts forms, and accept it once it has, even if a few dissenters remain and even if some individuals in it turn out to have erred.
B. you should accept the majority view at every stage.
C. you should never defer to a consensus, since science advances by dissent.
D. you should wait until dissent has disappeared entirely.

*Answer: A.*

**Q41.** A physicist with strong public views about immunology sits, on Anderson's ladder of expertise,

A. at the top, since physics is the most demanding science.
B. near the middle, because expertise in one technical field does not transfer.
C. at the bottom, with laypeople.
D. off the ladder entirely.

*Answer: B.*

## Day 9 — Social trust

**Q42.** Oreskes's account of why science is trustworthy rests on

A. the scientific method, applied uniformly across the sciences.
B. a social arrangement: a claim is handed to a community trained and professionally motivated to attack it, and only what survives is kept.
C. the value-neutrality of scientific practice.
D. peer review, understood as a check by two or three referees.

*Answer: B.*

**Q43.** Why is diversity among scientists supposed to help, given that the aim is consensus?

A. Because a diverse field is more popular with funders.
B. Because criticism only catches errors if the critics differ enough from each other to look in different places; a homogeneous community shares its blind spots.
C. Because diversity makes consensus easier to reach.
D. Because diversity of values eliminates value judgments.

*Answer: B.*

**Q44.** On values in science, Oreskes holds that

A. science should be value-free, and mostly is.
B. science should be value-free, though it never quite manages it.
C. value-free science is neither achievable nor desirable; what matters is that the values are visible and open to challenge.
D. values in science are always a form of bias.

*Answer: C.*

**Q45.** ★ Ravi follows only people who agree with him about housing policy and has never encountered an argument for the other side. A friend sends him a good piece against his view; he reads it and shifts his position. Nadia was told, long before she read any critic, that critics of her view are funded by developers. She is sent the same piece, looks up the author, finds a developer grant, and dismisses it. Ravi and Nadia are, respectively, in

A. an echo chamber and an epistemic bubble.
B. an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber.
C. both in epistemic bubbles.
D. both in echo chambers.

*Answer: B.* The diagnostic is not what they have been exposed to but what happens when the exposure arrives.

**Q46.** Evidential pre-emption, in Begby's sense, is

A. refusing to read sources from the other side.
B. being told in advance what the other side will say and why they will say it, so that when they say it, they appear to confirm the warning.
C. blocking outsiders from an informational network.
D. repeating a claim after it has been publicly refuted.

*Answer: B.* D is dialogic irrationality from Day 8, which is a reasonable thing to have to tell apart.

**Q47.** The pre-emptive warnings in the Overclock and Slow Down example work in large part because

A. they are outright fabrications that members cannot check.
B. they are not lies. Some academics really are threatened by the technology and some researchers really do hold equity, so the warning is plausible on its face.
C. members are forbidden from reading the other side.
D. the two channels never mention each other.

*Answer: B.*

**Q48.** ★ An echo chamber issues its own credentials, stages its own consensus, and teaches you to read a critic's honesty as evidence of bad faith. The significance of this, for the previous lecture, is that

A. Anderson's criteria are the wrong criteria.
B. the chamber does not prevent you from assessing whom to trust; it captures the assessment, turning each of Anderson's three checks to its own purposes.
C. echo chambers only form among people with no education.
D. consensus is never evidence of anything.

*Answer: B.* This and Q45 are the two that make the last two lectures into one argument rather than two topics.

**Q49.** Nguyen's proposed remedy for an echo chamber is

A. exposure to opposing views.
B. a social-epistemic reboot: suspending the whole network of trust and rebuilding it, rather than patching one belief at a time.
C. better fact-checking.
D. leaving social media.

*Answer: B.*

**Q50.** What happens to epistemic vigilance inside an echo chamber?

A. It shuts down; members stop checking anything.
B. It keeps running, but now reinforces the chamber, because the checks themselves have been captured.
C. It becomes more accurate.
D. It applies only to insiders.

*Answer: B.*

## Notes on the set

**The ★ set** is Q1, 4, 5, 9, 12, 14, 17, 19, 22, 27, 29, 31, 32, 36, 37, 45, 48 — seventeen questions, one or two from each lecture. It runs about fifteen minutes for a student who did the reading and rather longer for one who didn't, which is roughly the right shape for 2.5%.

**If you want a larger pool instead**, the other thirty-three are here to be drawn from, and Canvas will happily randomize within a group. Drawing three of five from each lecture would give a thirty-question quiz where no two students see quite the same paper, at the cost of making the item statistics harder to read.

**Most of these are one prompt away from a correct answer**, and that was always going to be true of an open-book multiple-choice quiz. The ones that survive being taken seriously anyway are the applied ones — Q14, Q19, Q37, Q45 — because a student who copies the answer without understanding gets nothing they can reuse, while a student who works it out has done the thing the lecture was for.

**Three questions are worth reading the distribution on**, whatever the mean looks like:

- Q9 (why illusions support the split). Picking A means the student has the conclusion without the argument.
- Q12 (cognitive penetration vs. choosing where to look). Picking C means they read the slide and missed the distinction it was set up to draw.
- Q14 (is a green emerald examined today grue?). Missing this means the definition never landed, and everything in Day 4 after the definition was noise for them.

**Day 1 is thin, and Q1–Q3 are syllabus compliance rather than philosophy.** That is a legitimate thing to spend three questions on in week five — Q1 in particular is worth having on record — but it should be a deliberate choice rather than an accident of the lecture range.

**Unbalanced by design.** Days 4, 6 and 9 get the most weight because they have the most that can be got wrong in an interesting way. Day 2 is under-weighted relative to its importance, on the grounds that validity and soundness will be examined repeatedly and do not need their whole share here.
