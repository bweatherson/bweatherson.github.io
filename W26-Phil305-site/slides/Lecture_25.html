Asymmetric Information

In many real situations, players differ in what they know:

  • A job applicant knows their own ability; the employer doesn’t.
  • A seller knows the quality of a used car; the buyer doesn’t.
  • A firm knows its own costs; rival firms don’t.

These are games of incomplete information or asymmetric information.

The Structure of a Signaling Game

A signaling game has:

  1. A Sender who has private information (their type).
  2. A Receiver who observes only the Sender’s message (signal), not their type.
  3. The Receiver takes an action based on what they observe.
  4. Both players receive payoffs that depend on type, message, and action.

The Receiver must use the message as evidence to infer the Sender’s type. This is where probability re-enters.

The Classic Example: Job Market Signaling

Spence’s Model (1973)

Workers are either high ability (H) or low ability (L). Employers can’t observe ability directly.

Workers can choose to get education (E) or not (N). Education is costly, but more costly for low-ability workers.

Employers observe the education choice and offer a wage based on what they infer.

The Signaling Game Tree

Nature
├── High (prob p)
│   └── Worker H
│       ├── E → Employer observes E → {High Wage, Low Wage}
│       └── N → Employer observes N → {High Wage, Low Wage}
└── Low (prob 1−p)
    └── Worker L
        ├── E → Employer observes E → {High Wage, Low Wage}
        └── N → Employer observes N → {High Wage, Low Wage}

The Employer sees E or N, but doesn’t see which branch of Nature they’re on.

The Key Insight

Suppose education is:

  • Costly for L workers (painful, time-consuming, expensive)
  • Less costly for H workers (comes naturally)

Then education can serve as a credible signal: not because it makes workers more productive, but because only high-ability workers find it worth the cost.

This is the logic of costly signaling: a signal is informative precisely because it’s too expensive for the low type to mimic.

Equilibria in Signaling Games

Separating Equilibrium

A separating equilibrium is one where different types send different messages.

In the education game:

  • H workers get education; L workers don’t.
  • Employers infer: E → High Wage, N → Low Wage.
  • H workers get education because the wage gain exceeds the cost.
  • L workers don’t get education because the cost exceeds the wage gain.

Everyone’s beliefs and actions are mutually consistent.

When is the Separating Equilibrium Stable?

For the separating equilibrium to hold:

  • H workers must prefer (E, High Wage) to (N, Low Wage): \(\text{High Wage} - c_H > \text{Low Wage}\)
  • L workers must prefer (N, Low Wage) to (E, High Wage): \(\text{High Wage} - c_L < \text{Low Wage}\)

This holds when \(c_H < c_L\) by enough. That is, when the cost difference is large enough that the signal can’t be profitably mimicked.

Pooling Equilibrium

A pooling equilibrium is one where all types send the same message.

In the education game:

  • Both H and L workers get education (or neither does).
  • Employers can’t learn anything from the signal. They pay a wage based on the prior probability of being high type.

In a pooling equilibrium, the signal carries no information.

Which Equilibrium Obtains?

Signaling games often have multiple equilibria. Both separating and pooling equilibria may exist simultaneously.

Which one we end up in depends on:

  • The cost structure (how different are \(c_H\) and \(c_L\)?).
  • Beliefs off the equilibrium path (what does the Receiver infer from unexpected messages?).
  • Refinements: additional criteria that rule out “unreasonable” equilibria.

A Troubling Implication

In Spence’s model, education may be:

  • A pure signal: costly, but adding no productivity.
  • Yet individually rational: workers get education to distinguish themselves.
  • And socially wasteful: resources spent on education that teaches nothing useful.

This is a striking application of game theory to economics and sociology. Whether it accurately describes real education markets is genuinely debated.

Cheap Talk

What If Signals Are Costless?

Suppose messages are free: words, not actions. Can communication convey information?

Crawford and Sobel (1982): Even when messages are costless, some information can be transmitted, but not all of it.

If the Sender and Receiver have perfectly aligned interests: full information transmission is possible.

If interests are partially opposed: only coarse information is credibly transmitted (“the value is in the upper half” but not the exact value).

Cheap Talk in Practice

  • A doctor advises a patient. Interests mostly aligned → credible communication.
  • A salesperson recommends a product. Interests diverge → be sceptical.
  • A politician makes a campaign promise. Interests may diverge → cheap talk: credible only if there are costs to breaking promises.

The analysis of cheap talk uses exactly the same equilibrium concepts but applied to costless messages.

Signaling Everywhere

Signaling games model:

  • Animal displays: peacock feathers, deer antlers (costly signals of genetic quality).
  • Warranties: a high-quality producer offers a long warranty (costly to low-quality imitators).
  • Conspicuous consumption: expensive purchases as signals of wealth.
  • Legal pleading: a strong case going to trial as a signal that the plaintiff won’t settle cheap.
