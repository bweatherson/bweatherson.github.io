Finding Your Strategy

A guide to the strategies in the Axelrod iterated prisoner's dilemma library — organized by philosophical approach, to help you pick one that you can actually explain.

Strategy Finder

Answer the questions below to be directed to a shortlist of strategies that match your approach. You can always browse the full reference table further down.

1. When you enter this game, what matters most to you?
Building mutual cooperation
I want to find a way to cooperate with my opponent and get us both to the good outcome — but I'll react if they betray me.
Rational self-interest
I think the rational thing to do is maximize my own score. Cooperation is a means, not an end.
Following a simple principle
I want a clean, principled strategy I can commit to and explain — like always cooperate, or copy what's working.
Going by the evidence
I cooperate or defect based on what the opponent's actual track record shows, not assumptions about what they'll do.
2. If your opponent defects on you, how do you respond?
Very forgiving
One defection barely changes anything. I cooperate unless I see a sustained pattern of betrayal.
Exact mirror
I copy what you did last round, every time. Cooperate with me and I cooperate; defect and I defect.
Punish, then forgive
I punish defection firmly — with several defections of my own — but I eventually reset and try again.
Never forgive
Once you've betrayed me, I'm done cooperating for good. I have a long memory and I don't give second chances.
2. What is your self-interest strategy?
Find and exploit cooperative players
Probe opponents to see if they'll let me get away with defecting. If they will, I'll keep going.
Always defect — no exceptions
The dominant strategy in a single-shot game is defect. I'm not going to complicate that.
Control the payoffs mathematically
Use zero-determinant strategies to force a relationship between our scores — I guarantee I come out ahead.
2. Which principle appeals to you most?
Repeat what worked; change what didn't
Known as Pavlov or Win-Stay Lose-Shift. If the last round earned me a good score, repeat my move; otherwise switch.
Always cooperate, no matter what
A principled commitment to cooperation, even at personal cost. Philosophically interesting, but risky.
Always defect, no matter what
The strategy that dominates the single-shot game. Does it still make sense when the game repeats?
2. How do you weigh the evidence?
Overall majority
Cooperate if the opponent has cooperated more than they've defected overall; otherwise defect.
Threshold: defect if defection rate exceeds X%
Cooperate with mostly-cooperative opponents; switch to defection once their defection rate is too high.
Suggested strategies — very forgiving
Tit for Two Tats axelrod.TitFor2Tats
Cooperates unless the opponent defected in both of the last two rounds. A single slip is ignored; sustained defection triggers a response.
Generous Tit for Tat axelrod.GTFT
Like Tit for Tat, but randomly forgives about one in ten defections. Avoids getting stuck in long punishment cycles.
Contrite Tit for Tat axelrod.ContriteTitForTat
Tracks whether its own defections were "accidental" (caused by noise). If it was the one to start a punishment cycle, it apologizes and cooperates until peace is restored.
Dynamic Two Tits for Tat axelrod.DynamicTwoTitsForTat
Punishes defections with two defections, but scales its forgiveness toward the opponent's overall cooperation rate — more cooperative opponents get more benefit of the doubt.
Suggested strategies — exact reciprocity
Tit for Tat axelrod.TitForTat
The famous winner of Axelrod's original tournament. Cooperates first, then copies the opponent's last move exactly. Simple, transparent, and hard to beat in a friendly field.
Omega Tit for Tat axelrod.OmegaTFT
A more sophisticated TFT that tracks whether the game has fallen into a deadlock cycle (alternating C and D) and cooperates to break it. Also measures the opponent's overall cooperativeness before retaliating.
Naive Prober axelrod.NaiveProber
Like Tit for Tat, but occasionally defects at random (about 5% of the time) to test whether the opponent will let it get away. If they retaliate, it returns to normal TFT.
Suggested strategies — punish then forgive
Two Tits for Tat axelrod.TwoTitsForTat
Punishes each single defection with two defections in a row, then returns to cooperating. Stricter than TFT, but still recoverable.
Soft Grudger axelrod.SoftGrudger
Punishes each defection with exactly four defections, then cooperates again. A proportional and forgiving version of Grim Trigger.
Gradual axelrod.Gradual
An escalating punisher: responds to the 1st defection with 1 defection, the 2nd with 2, and so on. After each punishment it cooperates for two rounds to signal readiness to make peace.
Hard Tit for Tat axelrod.HardTitForTat
Defects if the opponent defected at any point in the last three rounds. Stricter than plain TFT but not permanent — it will eventually return to cooperating.
Suggested strategies — unforgiving
Grudger (Grim Trigger) axelrod.Grudger
Cooperates until the first defection, then defects for the rest of the match with no possibility of forgiveness. The canonical grim trigger strategy from game theory.
Spiteful Tit for Tat axelrod.SpitefulTitForTat
Like Grudger, but requires two consecutive defections before locking into permanent retaliation. Harder to trigger accidentally.
Forgetful Grudger axelrod.ForgetfulGrudger
Grudger with a twist: after defecting for a while, it occasionally "forgets" the original betrayal and gives the opponent one more chance. Still very harsh.
Suggested strategies — test and exploit
Cooperator Hunter axelrod.CooperatorHunter
Cooperates for the first four rounds. If the opponent cooperated every single time, concludes they are a naive cooperator and defects for the rest of the match.
Prober axelrod.Prober
Opens with D, C, C. If the opponent cooperated in response (in rounds 2 and 3), classifies them as exploitable and defects forever. Otherwise, plays Tit for Tat.
Hard Prober axelrod.HardProber
A more aggressive version: opens with D, D, C, C. Same exploit-or-TFT logic. More likely to find victims, but more likely to provoke retaliation too.
Detective axelrod.Detective
Plays a fixed opener (C, D, C, C). If the opponent never retaliates, defects forever. Otherwise plays TFT. More elaborate probing sequence than Prober.
Always defect
Defector axelrod.Defector
Always defects, every round, against every opponent. The dominant strategy in a one-shot game. In a repeated game against a mixed field, it wins against cooperators but draws at mutual defection against everyone else.
Alternator axelrod.Alternator
Defects half the time by alternating C and D in a fixed pattern, regardless of the opponent. Not dominant, but interesting for what it does to pattern-detecting opponents.
Zero-determinant strategies
ZD Extort-2 axelrod.ZDExtort2
A zero-determinant extortion strategy. It uses a carefully chosen probability distribution over C and D to mathematically ensure that it earns twice as much as the opponent above the mutual defection baseline — no matter what the opponent does.
ZD Extort-4 axelrod.ZDExtort4
Like ZDExtort2 but more aggressive: earns four times as much above baseline. Pays less overall (since mutual cooperation is rare) but maintains a larger relative advantage.
ZD Generous Tit for Tat-2 axelrod.ZDGTFT2
A zero-determinant strategy tuned to be cooperative rather than exploitative. Forces both players toward mutual cooperation, but ensures the opponent earns slightly less than it does.
Note on ZD strategies The mathematics behind zero-determinant strategies (from Press & Dyson 2012) is beautiful but not trivial. You can use one without understanding all the math, but your explanation should engage with at least the basic idea: that certain mixed strategies can "control" the relationship between payoffs regardless of what the opponent does.
Win-Stay Lose-Shift (Pavlov) strategies
Win Stay Lose Shift axelrod.WinStayLoseShift
Also called Pavlov. Repeats the previous move if the round went well (mutual cooperation, or successfully exploiting a cooperator); switches if it went badly (was exploited, or mutual defection). Simple and often very effective.
Win Shift Lose Stay axelrod.WinShiftLoseStay
The exact opposite of Pavlov. Switches when things go well, stays when things go badly. An interesting philosophical contrast — what would justify this?
Always cooperate
Cooperator axelrod.Cooperator
Always cooperates, every round, no matter what. Will score well against other cooperators and poorly against defectors. The clearest test of a principled commitment to cooperation regardless of consequences.
Good for the assignment Cooperator is one of the easiest to explain philosophically — you can connect it to Kantian ethics, the Sermon on the Mount, or unconditional altruism. It will probably lose points in the tournament, which is itself philosophically interesting.
Always defect
Defector axelrod.Defector
Always defects. Dominates in a single-shot game, and beats naive cooperators in repeated play. But in a field of retaliators, it collects a lot of mutual-defection scores.
Suggested strategies — cooperate with the mostly-cooperative
Go By Majority axelrod.GoByMajority
Cooperates if the opponent has cooperated at least as often as they've defected over the whole match. Ties go to cooperation. Uses the full history.
Go By Majority (last 10) axelrod.GoByMajority10
Same logic, but only looks at the last 10 rounds. Allows opponents to "recover" from early defections.
Hard Go By Majority axelrod.HardGoByMajority
Same as GoByMajority, but ties go to defection. Slightly harsher — any equal history leads to defection rather than cooperation.
Suggested strategies — defect if defection rate is too high
Level Punisher axelrod.LevelPunisher
Cooperates for the first 10 rounds, then defects if the opponent's defection rate ever exceeds 20%. A tolerant strategy with a clear threshold.
Forgiving Tit for Tat axelrod.ForgivingTitForTat
Defects when the opponent's defection rate exceeds 10% and their most recent move was a defection. Otherwise cooperates.
Retaliate axelrod.Retaliate
Defects once the opponent has "won" (i.e., exploited it) more than 10% of the time. Measures exploitation directly rather than defection rate.

Full Strategy Reference

The library contains 230+ strategies. Below are the most comprehensible ones, grouped by family. Strategies marked advanced work fine but are hard to explain without engaging with the underlying mathematics or code.

nice Tit-for-Tat family

These strategies start by cooperating and respond to what the opponent does. They differ mainly in how long and how harshly they punish a defection. "Nice" strategies never defect first.

StrategyClass nameDescription
Tit for Tat axelrod.TitForTat Cooperates first; then copies the opponent's last move.
Tit for Two Tats axelrod.TitFor2Tats Only retaliates after two consecutive defections. Single slips are forgiven.
Two Tits for Tat axelrod.TwoTitsForTat Punishes each defection with two defections in a row.
Generous TFT axelrod.GTFT Like TFT but randomly forgives ~10% of defections.
Contrite TFT axelrod.ContriteTitForTat If its own defection started a retaliation cycle, it "apologizes" by cooperating until peace is restored.
Omega TFT axelrod.OmegaTFT Tracks both deadlock cycles and overall cooperativeness; adjusts tolerance accordingly.
Hard TFT axelrod.HardTitForTat Defects if the opponent defected at any point in the last three rounds.
Slow TFT axelrod.SlowTitForTwoTats2 Cooperates twice, then copies the opponent only if they played the same move twice in a row.
Suspicious TFT axelrod.SuspiciousTitForTat Like TFT but starts with a defection. "Nasty" — may defect first.
Naive Prober axelrod.NaiveProber TFT but occasionally defects at random (≈5%) to test the opponent's reaction.
nice Grudger / escalating punisher family

These strategies cooperate initially but punish defection harshly — sometimes permanently.

StrategyClass nameDescription
Grudger (Grim Trigger) axelrod.Grudger Cooperates until first defection; then defects forever. No forgiveness.
Spiteful TFT axelrod.SpitefulTitForTat Like Grudger but triggered by two consecutive defections, not one.
Soft Grudger axelrod.SoftGrudger Punishes with four defections in a row, then cooperates again.

mixed Win-Stay Lose-Shift (Pavlov) family

These strategies don't track what the opponent does directly. Instead they ask: did my last move pay off? If yes, repeat it. If no, switch. Simple reinforcement learning applied to a binary choice.

StrategyClass nameDescription
Win Stay Lose Shift axelrod.WinStayLoseShift The classic Pavlov strategy. "Good" outcomes: CC or DC (getting 3 or 5). "Bad" outcomes: CD or DD (getting 0 or 1).
Win Shift Lose Stay axelrod.WinShiftLoseStay The inverse of Pavlov. Philosophically puzzling — what reasoning would justify this?
nasty Probers and hunters

These strategies test opponents first, then decide how to treat them. They are "nasty" in the sense that they will exploit cooperative opponents if the test reveals they can get away with it.

advanced Advanced and machine-learning strategies

The library contains roughly 100 strategies in this category. They work, and you can use them, but explaining why they make the choices they do requires engaging with the underlying algorithms. These are suitable if you have a background in machine learning, evolutionary game theory, or are prepared to learn about one of these methods for the assignment.

FamilyExamplesWhat they are
Zero-Determinant ZDExtort2, ZDExtort4, ZDGTFT2, ZDGen2, ZDSet2, ZDMischief Strategies from Press & Dyson (2012) that use stochastic mixed strategies to "control" the linear relationship between both players' scores. Can enforce extortion or generosity.
Evolved neural nets EvolvedANN, EvolvedANN5, EvolvedANNNoise05 Strategies trained with machine learning against the Axelrod library. The weights are pre-specified; the behavior is opaque.
Evolved FSMs EvolvedFSM4, EvolvedFSM6, EvolvedFSM16, EvolvedHMM5 Finite state machines trained by evolutionary algorithms. Can be visualized as state diagrams but the design is not humanly interpretable.
Q-learning CautiousQLearner, RiskyQLearner, HesitantQLearner Strategies that learn during the tournament via reinforcement learning. Behavior depends on what they encounter.
Meta-strategies MetaWinner, MetaMajority, MetaMinority, MetaHunter Strategies that run many sub-strategies in parallel and follow whichever is currently doing best (or the majority vote, etc.).
PSO Gamblers & Lookers PSOGambler2_2_2, EvolvedLookerUp2_2_2 Lookup-table strategies trained with particle swarm optimization. Conditioned on the last 2 moves of each player.
Mathematical constants Golden, Pi, e Target a specific C:D ratio matching a mathematical constant. A bit of a joke, but philosophically discussable.

Writing Your Own Strategy in Python

If you know some Python, you can write an original strategy. A strategy in Axelrod is a Python class with a strategy(opponent) method that returns axelrod.Action.C or axelrod.Action.D. You can inspect the opponent's history (and your own) through the player objects. 

import axelrod

class MyStrategy(axelrod.Player):
    """A brief description of what this strategy does."""

    name = "My Strategy"
    classifier = {
        "memory_depth": 1,   # How many rounds back you look (float("inf") for unlimited)
        "stochastic": False,  # True if you use random numbers
        "long_run_time": False,
        "inspects_source": False,
        "manipulates_source": False,
        "manipulates_state": False,
    }

    def strategy(self, opponent: axelrod.Player) -> axelrod.Action:
        # opponent.history is a list of axelrod.Action.C and .D values
        # self.history is the same for your own moves
        if not opponent.history:
            return axelrod.Action.C  # First move
        if opponent.history[-1] == axelrod.Action.D:
            return axelrod.Action.D  # Retaliate
        return axelrod.Action.C      # Otherwise cooperate
For the assignment Writing your own strategy is permitted but not required. If you do, your explanation should describe the reasoning behind the design choices — not just what the code does.

What Makes a Good Explanation

The grade is primarily based on your written explanation, not your tournament score. Here are the kinds of questions a strong explanation should address:

  • What is the core principle? What values or assumptions does the strategy embody? What are you trying to do with this strategy?
  • Why is this rational? Why is this a sensible thing to try to do?
  • What are its vulnerabilities? When would this do badly, either in absolute or comparative terms? Why are you happy to accept this weakness?
  • What does it assume about the opponent? A natural way to design a strategy is to build something that does well agains some set of opponents. Who are the opponents you are thinking about when building your strategy?
  • How does it relate to ideas from the course? Does it try to build on Axelrod's ideas that strategies should be nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear, or does it aim for a virtue other than the ones he identified?
A note on tournament performance Tournament results are philosophically interesting data, not just scores. If your strategy does well, think about why — what features of the field favored it? If it does poorly, that's also philosophically interesting — does a strategy's poor performance mean it was wrong to choose it?
