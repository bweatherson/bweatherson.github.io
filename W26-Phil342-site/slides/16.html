A Question from Last Time

The Backward Induction Argument

Last time we left with a puzzle.

In a repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with a known endpoint, backward induction says you should defect in the last round, because there’s no future to protect.

But if you’ll defect in the last round, your opponent knows that. So they’ll defect in the second-to-last round. And so on, all the way back to round one.

Does this mean rational players can never cooperate?

Why It Doesn’t (Quite) Work That Way

Technically, the argument is valid for a finite, known-length game between perfectly rational players who know each other are perfectly rational.

But three things complicate this:

  • Players don’t actually reason that way in practice
  • If an opponent cooperates early, you have good reason to cooperate back
  • If the endpoint is uncertain, the unraveling argument fails entirely

The more interesting question: can we explain why cooperation emerges, and what strategies actually work?

Axelrod’s Experiment

Robert Axelrod

In 1980, Robert Axelrod, a political scientist here at the University of Michigan, ran an unusual experiment.

He invited researchers from economics, psychology, mathematics, sociology, and computer science to submit strategies for playing the iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma.

Each strategy was a computer program. Axelrod ran a round-robin tournament: every strategy against every other strategy, repeated many times. Whoever accumulated the most points overall won.

The First Tournament

The first tournament had 14 entries. The strategies ranged from simple to highly sophisticated. Some tried to detect and exploit patterns, some started with defection, some tried complex conditional logic.

The winner was the simplest program submitted: four lines of code.

It was submitted by the game theorist Anatol Rapoport, and it was Tit for Tat: cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever your opponent did last round.

The Second Tournament

Axelrod published the results and ran a second tournament. Now 62 entries came in.

People knew TFT had won. Some tried to beat it directly. Some submitted variants. Some entered highly sophisticated strategies designed to detect and outmaneuver it.

TFT won again.

Axelrod published The Evolution of Cooperation in 1984. It became one of the most widely cited books in the social sciences. It’s become very important in biology, as well as across PPE.

How the Tournament Worked

  • Each pair of strategies played 200 rounds of the Prisoner’s Dilemma
  • Every strategy faced every other strategy (round-robin)
  • Total score = points accumulated across all matches

How the Tournament Worked

The payoff matrix Axelrod used:

Cooperate Defect
Cooperate 3, 3 0, 5
Defect 5, 0 1, 1

Mutual cooperation earns 3 each. Mutual defection earns only 1 each. The temptation to defect is real, but exploitation backfires if the other player responds.

Note one key feature of this payoff structure. Alternating CD/DC does worse than continuous CC. Some people make this definitive of the game being a PD.

Reciprocators and Hunters

Reciprocators react to what their opponent does:

  • Tit for Tat: Cooperate first, then copy whatever the opponent did last round.
  • Generous TFT: Like TFT, but randomly forgives about one in ten defections.
  • Grim Trigger: Cooperates until the first defection — then defects forever. No second chances.

Pattern Hunters probe and exploit:

  • Cooperator Hunter: Tests whether the opponent has ever defected. If not, exploits them forever.
  • Prober: Opens with D, C, C to test for exploitability. Defects forever if the probe succeeds.
  • Cycle Hunter: Watches for any repeating pattern in the opponent’s moves, then defects once confirmed.

If You’re a Reciprocator…

You’ll be asked: How forgiving are you?

  • Very forgiving: Occasional defections don’t bother you. → Tit for Two Tats, Generous TFT
  • Standard: Mirror what your opponent does — cooperate for cooperate, defect for defect. → Tit for Tat, Omega TFT
  • Strict: Punish defection firmly and take a while to let it go. → Two Tits for Tat, Hard TFT, Soft Grudger
  • Unforgiving: Once betrayed, you never cooperate again. → Grim Trigger, Spiteful TFT

If You’re a Pattern Hunter…

You’ll be asked: What are you hunting for?

  • Exploitable cooperators: Find opponents who won’t fight back, and safely defect on them forever. → Cooperator Hunter, Prober, Hard Prober
  • Predictable patterns: Detect opponents playing a fixed, mechanical strategy and outmaneuver them. → Alternator Hunter, Cycle Hunter, Defector Hunter

What Makes Strategies Succeed

Axelrod’s Analysis

After the tournaments, Axelrod analyzed what separated the successful strategies from the unsuccessful ones.

He found that success didn’t come from being clever, aggressive, or unpredictable. It came from four surprisingly simple properties, plus a lesson about what counts as success.

Property 1: Be Nice

Never be the first to defect.

Of the top eight strategies in the second tournament, all eight were “nice”. They were never the first to defect.

None of the strategies that defected first finished in the top half.

This seems counterintuitive. Shouldn’t you gain an edge by defecting first?

Why Nice Strategies Win

A nasty strategy can do well once, but it then plays the rest of the game in a damaged state.

Once you’ve defected first, your opponent retaliates. Now you’re both locked into defecting, earning 1 per round instead of 3.

A nice strategy avoids this entirely. Against another nice strategy, it earns 3 per round for all 200 rounds. That adds up.

Rules Matter

Let’s say we ran the same tournament, but didn’t add up points. Instead we treated everything like a basketball game, and asked who won each pairing.

What’s the best strategy to play in that game?

It’s All D. It wins every time. But it’s often the worst strategy using the rules as we’ve defined them.

This matters for thinking about what real world situations can be modeled this way.

Property 2: Be Retaliatory

Respond to defection quickly.

Being nice doesn’t mean being a pushover. Strategies that never defected (always cooperate) got exploited by anything that defected repeatedly, earning 0 per round instead of 3.

TFT retaliates immediately: defect on me this round, I defect back next round. No delay, no forgiveness yet.

This makes exploitation unprofitable from the start.

Property 3: Be Forgiving

Return to cooperation after you’ve punished.

Strategies that retaliated but never forgave got locked into mutual defection loops. They earn 1 per round for the rest of the match. That’s almost as bad as being exploited.

TFT forgives immediately: as soon as the opponent cooperates, TFT cooperates on the next move.

Quick retaliation plus quick forgiveness keeps punishment short and gets both players back to earning 3 per round.

Property 4: Be Clear

Make your strategy legible to your opponent.

TFT is easy to understand: cooperate first, then mirror whatever your opponent just did. Any opponent can figure this out within a few rounds.

Many sophisticated strategies were essentially unreadable. Opponents couldn’t tell if they were dealing with something cooperative or exploitative.

When opponents can’t figure you out, they defect defensively, and you lose points you could have made through cooperation.

Don’t Be Envious

Axelrod noticed that many people framed the tournament wrong: they tried to beat their opponents. Taking that to an extreme, you might play All D.

But TFT never beats any individual opponent. Against a defector it breaks even; against a cooperator it ties; it only “loses” when the opponent defects first.

The goal isn’t to beat your opponent. It’s to do well across all your matches.

A strategy that earns 3 per round against many opponents accumulates more points than one that “wins” against a few while earning 1 per round against everyone else.

The Deeper Point

Cooperation can emerge from self-interest alone: without altruism, central authority, or communication.

All that’s required:

  • The interaction repeats (there’s a “shadow of the future”)
  • Players can recognize each other’s behavior and respond to it
  • A strategy that rewards cooperation and punishes defection is available

Axelrod showed this works even in evolutionary settings: if successful strategies spread and unsuccessful ones die out, cooperative strategies dominate over time, even starting from a world of defectors.

For Next Time

Axelrod’s work raises as many questions as it answers:

  • Does TFT always win, or does it depend on who else is in the field?
  • What happens to cooperation in large populations over time?
  • How does this apply beyond game theory, to arms races, trade, and everyday trust?
