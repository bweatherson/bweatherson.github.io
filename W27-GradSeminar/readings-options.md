## Background theme: structured and unstructured propositions (3 Nov)

Not a session of its own. Introduce on Jan 15 and come back to it in discussion.

- Kaplan: structured contents, so an indexical can put its referent straight into the proposition. Lewis and Stalnaker: unstructured contents, where that task doesn't exist. (Stalnaker's haecceities set aside.)
- Perry's notion of an unarticulated constituent assumes propositions have constituents. In an unstructured framework the question can only be whether content depends on context with no expression responsible.
- Binding argument: in either framework it is evidence about compositional semantic value. It shows directly that the proposition has a slot to fill only if propositional structure tracks logical form, as on King's view.
- Kaplan is structured but has times in the circumstance, so structure on its own does not settle what goes in the index.
- King 2003 makes more sense given his deeper commitments (King, *The Nature and Structure of Content*, 2007, ch. 6, "Tense, Modality, and Propositions"; background on Feb 26, and a possible presenter reading).
- Second axis to keep in view: Lewis's de se contents ("Attitudes De Dicto and De Se", 1979). Several relativists and judge theorists use centered contents (Egan 2007; Stephenson's world-time-individual context sets).
- Places it comes up: Feb 5 (Perry), Feb 19 (Recanati, Cappelen and Lepore), Feb 26 (King), Apr 9 (Ninan 2010, optional), Apr 23 (MacFarlane's mostly Lewisian framework).
- Worlds in the index, not the content (Lewis and most others; Stone, and Cappelen and Hawthorne's "original sin" diagnosis, as dissenters). Modal realism plus indexical actuality means Lewis has no monadic truth for propositions; an actualist like Stalnaker can define truth simpliciter as truth at the actual world. But Lewis's preference for divergence over branching (*On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986) means every context sits in exactly one world, which blocks MacFarlane's 2003 route to assessment sensitivity for future contingents. Worth raising on Feb 26, where the relevant passage of Lewis 1986 is optional.

## Apr 16: the roulette case instead of the miners (4 Nov)

Someone plans to give $1000 to charity. Passing a casino, they could bet it on one number of the next spin and give whatever they win. They should not bet: the bet has negative expected value. But whichever number comes up, betting on that number gives more to charity than $1000. Same structure as the miners case, with no life-and-death stakes and no group agent.

Things to watch:
- The antecedent of "if it lands on 17, you ought to bet on 17" is a future chance event; in the miners case it is a settled fact that nobody knows. Possibly useful (ties to MacFarlane 2003 on Feb 26), but an objector could say the two kinds of conditional behave differently. A settled-fact version would separate the issues.
- The expected-value gap is small (house edge of about 2.7% on a single-zero wheel, about 5.3% on a double-zero wheel). Fine if "should not bet" reads as a clear verdict; if students treat it as near-indifference, worsen the odds.

Revised version (4 Nov): bet on something already settled but not yet announced, such as an Oscar category after voting has closed, and make the only available book one with a large house cut, so every bet has low expected return. Stipulate that the person's credences match the market (no inside information); otherwise some bet could have positive expected value for them. Bonus: the vote counters already know the result, so the question of whose information "ought" is relative to comes up the same way it did for epistemic modals on Apr 2.
