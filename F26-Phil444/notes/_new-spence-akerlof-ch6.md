## Spence and the Value of a Useless Signal

**College Signal** is not a toy invented for this chapter. It is, in essentials, the model @spence1973 published as an account of the labour market, and which won him a share of a Nobel Prize.

It is worth being explicit about what is startling in it, because the formal work above can make it look tamer than it is.

In the separating equilibrium, bright students go to college and dull ones do not, employers hire college-goers and reject the rest, and everyone is behaving perfectly rationally. Notice what is doing the work. **Nothing in the model says that college teaches anyone anything.** The student's productivity is settled by their type, which Nature fixed before the game started. College does not change it. All college does is cost less to a bright student than to a dull one.

That asymmetry in cost is the entire mechanism. Because the bright student finds college cheaper, there is a range of prices at which the bright student is willing to pay and the dull one is not, and paying it therefore reveals the type. The signal works because it is expensive, and because it is differentially expensive. It does not need to be useful.

This is an uncomfortable result, and it is uncomfortable in two directions.

Taken as a description, it suggests that a large part of what education is for might be sorting rather than teaching, and that the private return to a degree can substantially exceed its social return. Taken as a normative matter, it describes a lot of wasted effort. In the separating equilibrium the bright students collectively spend a great deal on college, and the only thing they get for it is to be distinguished from the dull ones, which is information the employer would have been happy to have for free.

Two cautions before that gets overstated. The model says nothing about whether college in fact teaches anything; it says that a signalling equilibrium can exist even if it does not. Both things can be true at once, and separating the two empirically is difficult. And the argument that signalling is wasteful assumes the sorting itself has no value, which is false if matching the right people to the right jobs is worth something.

The general lesson is worth keeping. When one side of a transaction knows something the other does not, costly and otherwise pointless actions can become rational, precisely because they are costly.

## Akerlof and the Market for Lemons

@spence1973 shows what the informed side can do about asymmetric information. @akerlof1970 asks what happens when they cannot do anything about it, and the answer is that the market can disappear.

The setting is used cars. Sellers know whether their car is good or a lemon. Buyers do not. Suppose, to keep the arithmetic simple, that there are equal numbers of each, and the values are these.

+:------------+:--------------:+:-------------:+
|             | **To seller**  | **To buyer**  |
+-------------+----------------+---------------+
| **Good car**| 100            | 120           |
+-------------+----------------+---------------+
| **Lemon**   | 40             | 60            |
+-------------+----------------+---------------+

Every car is worth more to a buyer than to its seller, so if everyone could see quality, every car would change hands and everyone would be better off.

Now suppose buyers cannot tell the two apart. A buyer who thinks the odds are even values a random car at $\frac{120 + 60}{2} = 90$, and so is willing to pay up to 90.

But a good car is worth 100 to its owner. At 90, no owner of a good car sells. So the only cars on the market are lemons, and the buyer who works this out revises: a car actually on offer is worth 60. Buyers now offer at most 60. Lemon owners, who value their cars at 40, are happy to sell.

The market clears, but only lemons trade, at 60. Good cars never change hands, even though every good-car sale would have produced a surplus of 20.

The unravelling is the point. Buyers' willingness to pay depends on the average quality of the cars on offer, but the average quality of the cars on offer depends on what buyers will pay. Lower the price and you drive out the better cars, which lowers the average quality, which lowers the price. With a continuous range of qualities rather than two, the process can run all the way down and the market can vanish entirely.

Notice how different the failure is from anything we have seen. This is not a coordination problem, and it is not a Prisoners' Dilemma. Nobody is tempted to defect and nobody is trying to be unpredictable. Every player behaves perfectly reasonably given what they know. The trade that fails to happen is one that both parties would have wanted, and no rearrangement of their incentives will produce it, because the problem is what they know rather than what they want.

It is also worth seeing that this is the same structure as **College Signal** with the signalling removed. In both, one side knows their type and the other does not. Spence's students have an action available that separates the types. Akerlof's sellers do not, and without one the good types are driven out of the market by the bad. That is why warranties, certification schemes, brand reputation, and professional licensing exist: they are institutions for manufacturing the separating action that the bare market does not supply.
