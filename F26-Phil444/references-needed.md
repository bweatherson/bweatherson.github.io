## G. The Reep and Hughes source

Almost certainly Jonathan Wilson, *Inverting the Pyramid*. Wilson's account has the
argument the chapter 2 example is built on: Reep found about 80% of goals came from
moves of three passes or fewer, but about 91.5% of all moves were three passes or
fewer, so the short sequences were in fact the less productive kind. Wilson is also
where Reep and Charles Hughes get paired, Hughes being the FA's director of
coaching who turned the analysis into a direct-play doctrine.

```bibtex
@book{wilson2008,
  author    = {Wilson, Jonathan},
  title     = {Inverting the Pyramid: The History of Football Tactics},
  publisher = {Orion},
  address   = {London},
  year      = {2008},
  isbn      = {978-0-7528-8995-5}
}
```

Three cautions.

- **Editions.** There are several. The 2008 Orion first edition is above; there are
  Orion reissues in 2013 and later, and the US edition is retitled *The History of
  Soccer Tactics* (Nation Books). Page references will not carry across, so cite
  whichever one is on your shelf.
- **No page number here.** I have not seen the book, so I cannot tell you where the
  Reep material sits, and the 80% and 91.5% figures above are from a secondary
  source reporting Wilson rather than from Wilson directly. Check them against your
  copy before quoting.
- **Two different Hughes.** Charles Hughes is the FA coach in Wilson's story. Mike
  Hughes is a sports scientist who, with Ian Franks, published "Analysis of passing
  sequences, shots and goals in soccer" (2005), which is a *critique* of the Reep
  line. Easy to conflate when searching, and citing the wrong one would invert the
  point.

If you use Wilson's real numbers instead of the invented ones, the example gets
better rather than worse: the arithmetic still shows the fallacy, and it stops being
hypothetical.
