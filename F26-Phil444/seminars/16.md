The handout (`16-sen-handout`) has every definition from Ch 1\*, 2\* and A1\*,
so the apparatus does not need to be read out. Tell them at the start that it is
a reference sheet for the next six weeks rather than a set of things to learn

# The four pieces of apparatus we actually use today

Introduce these through the exercise in §3 rather than before it. Each takes
about one line once they have seen a case.

1. **Quasi-ordering**: reflexive and transitive, possibly incomplete.
2. **Maximal vs best**: $x$ is maximal if nothing beats it; $x$ is best if it
   beats or ties everything. $C(S,R) \subseteq M(S,R)$, and the gap is the whole
   story when $R$ is incomplete.
3. **The Pareto relation** $\bar{R}$: $x\bar{R}y$ iff everyone weakly prefers
   $x$; $x\bar{P}y$ iff in addition someone strictly prefers it.
4. **Collective choice rule**: any map from a profile of individual orderings to
   one social preference relation. Note what is *not* required: the social
   relation need not be an ordering, or complete, or transitive. That is the
   difference between Definition 2\*1 and Arrow's social welfare function, and
   it is the room Sen is giving himself.

# Exercise A: the incompleteness audit

Put this profile on the board or on a slip of paper. A city budget, four
options, three residents, strict rankings top to bottom.

| | Ana | Ben | Cal |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| 1st | $x$ | $y$ | $w$ |
| 2nd | $y$ | $w$ | $x$ |
| 3rd | $w$ | $x$ | $y$ |
| 4th | $z$ | $z$ | $z$ |

: $w$ = current budget; $x$ = more transit, funded by parking fees; $y$ = more
parks, funded by a property tax; $z$ = tax cut with service reductions

**Question 1: which pairs does the Pareto relation rank?**

Six pairs. Three of them involve $z$, which is everyone's last choice, so
$w\bar{P}z$, $x\bar{P}z$, $y\bar{P}z$. The other three are all incomparable:
Ana and Cal disagree about $x$ vs $w$, Ana and Ben disagree about $x$ vs $y$,
Cal disagrees with both others about $y$ vs $w$.

So $\bar{R}$ ranks half the pairs, and every pair it ranks involves the one
option nobody wants. Among the live options it says nothing. This is Sen's
remark on p. 68 made concrete: "Precisely how incomplete the Pareto criterion
will be depends on how unanimous the individuals are."

**Question 2: what is the Pareto-optimal set?**

$\{w, x, y\}$. Three maximal elements and no best element. Worth pausing on:
Lemma 2\*e guarantees at least one Pareto-optimal state over a finite set, and
that is all it guarantees. It gives you maximality, never bestness. Students who
have met the phrase "Pareto-optimal" elsewhere usually think it picks out
something; here it rules out one option in four.

**Question 3: what would have to be true for $\bar{R}$ to be complete?**

Let them work at it before giving Lemma 2\*b: whenever anyone strictly prefers
$x$ to $y$, everyone must weakly prefer $x$ to $y$. Sen's footnote 2 on p. 68
puts it as the near-identical-preferences case. The opposite extreme, footnote
3, is two people with strictly opposed orderings, where nothing is comparable at
all.

# Sen's complaint about Pareto-optimality

Once they have seen that Pareto-optimality rules out very little, the objection
in §2.1 lands.

- An economy can be Pareto-optimal "even when some people are rolling in luxury
  and others are near starvation as long as the starvers cannot be made better
  off without cutting into the pleasures of the rich."
- The Nero line: if preventing the burning of Rome would have made Nero feel
  worse off, then letting him burn Rome would have been Pareto-optimal.
- > In short, a society or an economy can be Pareto-optimal and still be
  > perfectly disgusting.

This is the sentence to have on the board. It is also the first place in the
book where Sen's objection is about the *informational base* rather than about
consistency, which connects back to the thread from Class 15.

# Pareto-inclusive rules, and the method of majority decision

§2.2, briefly, and mostly as setup.

- A CCR is **Pareto-inclusive** if it subsumes the Pareto relation and possibly
  goes beyond it ($\bar{R}$ is a subrelation of $R$).
- The method of majority decision is Pareto-inclusive, and is *decisive* in
  Sen's sense of always yielding a complete ranking. It buys that completeness
  with intransitivity, as Condorcet showed.
- The Pareto criterion taken on its own is a CCR, but not a decisive one.
- So there is a trade: the Pareto rule is consistent and nearly silent; majority
  rule is noisy and inconsistent. Everything in the next six weeks is an attempt
  to get something in between.

**Weak and strong Pareto.** On p. 70 Sen separates the version where everyone
strictly prefers $x$ ($\bar{\bar{P}}$, Definition 2\*4) from the usual version
where someone strictly prefers and the rest are at least indifferent ($\bar{P}$).
Useful five-minute exercise: construct a profile where they differ (one person
indifferent, everyone else prefers $x$) and ask which rule should govern. The
payoff is that an impossibility theorem assuming only $\bar{\bar{P}}$ is a
stronger result, because it assumes less.

Sen's own hesitation is worth quoting, since students tend to treat the Pareto
principle as beyond question:

> If everyone in the society wants $x$ rather than $y$, in which sense can
> society prefer $y$ to $x$, or even be indifferent or undecided? This is a
> fairly compelling argument, but I would doubt that it is altogether
> unexceptionable.

His reason: the social choice over $x$ and $y$ may depend on individual
preferences over *other* pairs, which is exactly the Independence condition
being questioned. He postpones it to Ch 6.

# Unanimity as a decision rule

§2.3. Buchanan and Tullock, quoted at p. 96:

> The individualistic theory of the constitution we have been able to develop
> assigns a central role to a single decision-making rule — that of general
> consensus or unanimity. The other possible rules for choice-making are
> introduced as variants from the unanimity rule.

The problem Sen poses: unanimity does not exist on most questions, so what do we
do when the rule is silent? Buchanan and Tullock's answer, and Sen's statement
of it:

> Given that some prefer an alternative $x$ to the status quo $y$ and no one
> regards $x$ to be worse than $y$, $x$ is socially preferred to $y$; and when
> this condition is not satisfied the status quo $y$ is preferred to the other
> alternative $x$.

And Sen's verdict: "This method is one of supreme conservatism." Marie
Antoinette's opposition to the First Republic would have saved the monarchy, and
"the world would have seen very little change."

# The default is doing all the work

This is the main discussion point, and the one I want to make sure we hit.

**The unanimity condition is symmetric.** $x\bar{P}y$ and $y\bar{P}x$ are the
same shape. Nothing in the Pareto relation favours the status quo. The
conservatism enters in the second clause, which resolves every case the Pareto
relation leaves open by designating one alternative as the winner by default.
So "supreme conservatism" is not a property of unanimity. It is a property of a
particular way of completing an incomplete relation.

**Which raises the obvious question: could we default the other way?** A rule on
which *non-changes* require unanimity. Stay at $y$ only if everyone agrees $y$
is at least as good as $x$ and someone strictly prefers it; otherwise move.

Two things go wrong, and they are different from each other.

*It does not say where to go.* With three or more options, if several are
Pareto-incomparable to the status quo, the rule says do not stay and is silent
on the destination. Run it on the Exercise A profile from $w$ and it tells you
to leave $w$ for either $x$ or $y$, with no basis for choosing. By Definition
2\*1 that disqualifies it as a collective choice rule: a CCR has to deliver one
social preference relation.

*It does not stop.* Once you move to $x$, $x$ is the status quo, and you move
again unless $x$ strictly Pareto-dominates everything. The rule terminates only
at a Pareto-*best* state, and Lemma 2\*e delivers only a Pareto-*maximal* one.
The sharpest version: if everyone is indifferent between $v$ and $w$, then
neither $v\bar{P}w$ nor $w\bar{P}v$, so the rule oscillates between them
forever. Unanimous indifference is enough to break it.

**The honest statement of the asymmetry.** Sen's rule is determinate in the
generic case because it always says "stay", and a status quo that nothing
Pareto-dominates is the normal situation. The inverted rule is indeterminate in
the generic case because it always says "go". Same structural feature, opposite
sign. The conservatism and the determinacy are the same property.

Worth being careful here: Sen's rule as stated is a rule about a status quo and
a single proposal. Run it on the Exercise A profile with $z$ as the status quo
and it tells you to leave $z$ without telling you where to go, which is the same
defect. The difference is that this case is unusual and the inverted rule's case
is normal.

**So there are three defaults, not two.** This is the structure for the rest of
the chapter and for Ch 5.

| Default when $\bar{R}$ is silent | Character | Where it goes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Status quo wins | Determinate, conservative | Buchanan and Tullock, §2.3 |
| Challenger wins | Indeterminate, non-terminating | Not a CCR |
| Declare social indifference | Determinate, non-conservative | The Pareto-extension rule, Ch 5 and 5\* |

Sen names the third himself in §2.2, and does not like it:

> A particularly simple way of extending the Pareto relation is to declare all
> Pareto-optimal points as indifferent. This will amount to a deliberate
> exclusion of distributional considerations. I cannot believe that this will
> appeal to many people, but I do not doubt that it might appeal to some.

The chapter closes by promising "a set of axioms that are necessary and
sufficient for this rather arbitrary rule" in Ch 5\*. And the Pareto-extension
rule is where Gibbard's oligarchy result bites, since it gives every person a
veto. So the trichotomy is also a map of where we are going.

# Exercise B: run all three defaults

Same profile as Exercise A. Three groups, one default each, five minutes.

- **Status quo wins.** Try each of $w$, $x$, $y$ as the starting point. In all
  three cases nothing unanimously beats the status quo, so you stay. The outcome
  is whatever you started with. The preferences did no work at all. Start from
  $z$ and you learn only that you should leave.
- **Challenger wins.** From any of $w$, $x$, $y$ you must move, with no
  destination specified. Add a fifth option $v$ that everyone ranks exactly
  where they rank $w$, and the rule cycles between $v$ and $w$ without end.
- **Declare indifference.** $w$, $x$ and $y$ become socially indifferent, all
  socially better than $z$. The choice set is $\{w, x, y\}$: determinate as a
  set, silent about which. And Ana alone can prevent anything being declared
  socially better than $x$.

Then the question to put to the room: **the three rules share a unanimity
condition and differ only in the default, and they have completely different
political characters. So which part of the rule is the political commitment?**

If there is appetite, the follow-up is that the designated default need not be
the status quo at all. A sunset clause designates a reversion. An appropriations
lapse designates a shutdown, made deliberately painful to force agreement. The
general point is that whoever sets the default is making the decision, and
unanimity is the part that looks neutral.

# Blocking, taking, and the smokey factory

Sen's footnote 6 on p. 72 is the place where he comes closest to the point in §7,
and it is worth putting in front of them directly.

- Buchanan and Tullock note the "paradoxical result that the rule of unanimity
  is the same as the minority rule of one", and defend it by distinguishing the
  power of *taking* action from the power of *blocking* action. Unanimity gives
  each person only the latter.
- Sen is doubtful. The distinction "represents the difference between the power
  *to impose* external costs on others and the power *to prevent* external costs
  from being imposed", and which is which depends on what the action is.
- His two cases: compulsory road repair lost for lack of unanimous support, and
  an antipollution measure blocked by the owner of a smokey factory.
- The second one is the argument. The factory owner's block is not the
  prevention of an imposition. It is the continuation of one. Where the status
  quo is itself an ongoing harm, the blocker is taking action, and the
  taking/blocking distinction collapses.

So the defence of the unanimity rule turns on a description of the status quo as
neutral, and the status quo is not neutral.

# Two qualifications Sen enters

Both from the end of §2.3, both good discussion prompts.

**Logrolling.** Buchanan and Tullock argue that unanimity can emerge where none
existed, through compromise and trade: "I can eschew my mild opposition to your
proposal in return for your support for mine about which I feel more strongly."
Sen grants the point but observes that a CCR is defined over complete
descriptions of social states, which already include logrolling compromises. So
the Buchanan-Tullock reply shows that individual orderings over *complete
packages* must be largely unanimous, which is a much stronger claim than it
sounds.

**Acceptance is not endorsement.** What compromises people accept depends on
their bargaining power.

> A labourer in a monopsonistic labour market may accept certain terms of
> agreement feeling that he cannot hope to get anything better, but this does
> not mean that it is unanimously preferred to an alternative set of terms.

Sen's conclusion: "the general acceptance of a compromise solution should not be
interpreted as universal endorsement." Worth asking whether this is a point
about preference formation (adaptive preferences, which he develops later) or a
point about what unanimity measures.

# If we have time

**The forward link to bargaining.** The designated status quo in a unanimity
rule is the disagreement point. Chapters 8 and 8\* are about how the
disagreement point determines the bargaining outcome, which is the same
observation as §7 in a different vocabulary. We have the equipment for this from
the game theory half, so it is available if the discussion goes that way.

**Why this is a chapter about incompleteness rather than about unanimity.**
Everything in Ch 2 follows from $\bar{R}$ being a quasi-ordering rather than an
ordering. If it were complete there would be no default clause to argue about.
This is Sen's second departure from Arrow's framework, from the Ch 1 §1.2 list
we did last time.
